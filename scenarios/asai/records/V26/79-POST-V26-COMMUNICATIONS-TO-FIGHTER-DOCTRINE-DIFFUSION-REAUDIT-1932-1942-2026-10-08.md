# POST-V26 communications-to-fighter-doctrine diffusion re-audit, 1932-1942 — 2026-10-08

Status: WORKING-CLOSE / INSTITUTIONAL-DOCTRINE RE-AUDIT / NO COMBAT OUTCOME PROMOTION

## Authority guard

V26 canon remains 1941-12-07T14:30:00-10:30 HST.

This file supersedes the overly restrictive reading of V26:77-78.

V26:77 was correct that:
- radio hardware alone does not magically create doctrine;
- EMI suppression is not the same thing as modern GCI.

It was too restrictive in treating fighter formation/control doctrine as essentially historical-static after years of improved communications and continuous war.

The correct model is:
technical reliability -> repeated use -> procedure -> training -> combat feedback -> institutionalization.

No "thought reform" or future doctrine import is required.

## 1. Closed technical origin

### 1932Q4-1933H1 — the noise problem becomes experimentally obvious

E4 GT aircraft provide a clean comparison:
- no continuous piston ignition spark train after start;
- much quieter radio environment than piston aircraft using similar radio/antenna equipment.

Japanese engineers already know RF interference exists.
The divergence is that they receive an unusually persuasive military aircraft comparison early.

Effect:
- the problem becomes measurable;
- "bad radio" is decomposed into radio-set performance versus installation/ignition/power noise.

### 1933H2-1934 — acceptance engineering

Working authority already closes:
- aircraft-side radio-noise acceptance-test proposal;
- magneto / HT lead / plug shielding;
- bonding;
- generator suppression;
- condensers/chokes/filtering;
- power filtering;
- connector/weather/vibration work.

This turns EMI from a crew complaint into a qualification item.

### 1935-1936 — installation reliability enters ordinary military practice

Important aircraft begin receiving acceptance requirements.
The process spreads especially into:
- reconnaissance;
- bomber / long-range;
- naval aviation.

At this stage the main effect is still technical:
- more calls are intelligible;
- more aircraft have repeatable installations;
- weak-signal communication is less often lost to ignition noise.

But the organizational consequence begins:
if the radio now works often enough, commanders can start writing procedures that assume it may work.

## 2. 1937 war changes the bottleneck

The V7 authority explicitly says:

"radio alone does not fix doctrine."

It also says China combat exposes:
- procedure problems;
- organization problems;
rather than simply proving "radio does not work."

That distinction is decisive.

From mid/late 1937 onward:
the limiting problem increasingly shifts from:
"can the pilot hear anything?"
toward:
"what is he supposed to report, to whom, in what format, and what does the receiving commander do with it?"

Therefore wartime adaptation is expected.

## 3. 1937H2-1938 — first procedural fighter/air-control adaptation

Do not create a modern fighter doctrine overnight.

Plausible and now WORKING-CLOSE developments:

### Communication procedure
Naval and Army aviation separately standardize more of:
- call signs;
- acknowledgement;
- contact / no-contact report;
- rough enemy number;
- rough altitude band;
- bearing / course;
- fuel / return / emergency calls;
- rendezvous / reform calls.

Exact phraseology remains OPEN and service-specific.

### Leader responsibility
The section/chutai leader increasingly becomes:
- the principal radio speaker;
- the element that receives broad vector/mission changes;
- the source of group status back to the controller.

Wingmen are not transformed into individually controlled assets.

### Formation consequence
The three-aircraft Japanese fighter formation is retained.

Radio reliability allows:
- somewhat more willingness to open spacing before contact;
- deliberate split/reform by leader command;
- easier reassembly after a firing pass;
- reduced dependence on continuous visual imitation of every leader maneuver.

This is an evolutionary change inside the existing shotai/chutai concept.

It is NOT:
- automatic adoption of U.S.-style two-plane doctrine;
- four-plane finger-four;
- continuous individual radio control.

## 4. 1938 operational feedback makes procedure valuable

The existing worldline 1938 airpower replay already shows:
- faster local command response from fresher reconnaissance;
- fewer stale-information attacks;
- recurring recon -> strike -> BDA -> retask cycles;
- high-cover tactics affecting conventional-aircraft losses;
- command/liaison/rapid-transport missions becoming routine.

These are evidence that the military is learning to exploit information that arrives reliably.

For fighter forces, the analogous institutional lesson is:
**communications are useful only if task ownership and report format are pre-briefed.**

By late 1938 the working fighter-direction culture should therefore include:
- assigned patrol/escort responsibility;
- leader-to-leader reporting;
- planned reform point / altitude after combat;
- explicit return/relief procedure;
- avoidance of several sections unknowingly performing the same task when communication remains available.

This reduces avoidable coordination failure.
It does not eliminate aggressive individual pursuit after visual combat begins.

## 5. 1939 — training and readiness become procedural rather than ad hoc

By 1939 the worldline has had:
- several years of more reliable aviation radio installations;
- roughly two years of continuous China combat feedback.

Working doctrinal development:

### Fighter radio discipline
Training increasingly includes:
- leader speaks / wingmen keep channel clear unless necessary;
- short standard reports;
- prebriefed code words / sectors / altitude bands;
- acknowledgement before major retask where practical;
- regroup / lost-leader procedure.

### Formation attack
Do NOT invent "formation firing."

Instead improve:
- attack sequencing;
- target allocation;
- cover assignment;
- reform after pass.

A three-plane shotai may prebrief:
- leader attacks;
- one wingman follows/supports;
- one wingman watches high/rear,
or another equivalent local arrangement.

The exact geometry remains leader/unit dependent.

### Command
Base/ship/controller functions increasingly operate on:
- group/shotai identity;
- sector;
- approximate altitude;
- task;
rather than attempting to control individual aircraft.

This is technically compatible with the available information system.

## 6. 1940 — A6M combat use arrives into an already-changed communications culture

A6M does not enter a 1933 communications environment.

By its 1940 combat use:
- radio-noise standards have existed for years;
- naval aviation has years of war experience;
- fighter leaders and controllers have learned that usable voice communication changes what can be attempted.

Therefore worldline A6M tactical use should differ from a historical-stock "radio mostly distrusted" reading.

### Retain
- three-plane shotai as the basic formal/tactical unit;
- nine-plane chutai structure;
- Japanese preference for aggressive offensive air combat;
- high pilot skill / visual tactical initiative.

### Change
The formation becomes more **radio-assisted**:
- leader can order split / reform;
- leader can call target-type / direction changes;
- section can maintain looser spacing with less fear of permanent separation;
- high cover / attacking element assignment is easier to preserve;
- sequential firing passes can be reordered by leader call;
- fuel / damage / return state can be communicated instead of inferred only visually.

Radio improves execution of tactics the Japanese already understand.
It does not require importing a foreign tactical philosophy.

## 7. Late 1940 — carrier concentration provides a fleet-level forcing function

Historical Japanese naval aviation already learned from China that carrier air mass matters and moved toward concentrated carrier operations by the end of 1940.

In this worldline, more usable fighter radios interact with that institutional development.

Working fleet-air consequence:
- carrier divisions prebrief common fighter call signs / frequencies;
- CAP sectors and altitude bands can be assigned across the division;
- a composite CAP leader can coordinate several shotai at group level;
- one section/chutai can be ordered to remain high or in reserve while another engages.

Do NOT infer:
- centralized individual fighter tracking;
- perfect cross-carrier common picture;
- seamless frequency management.

The gain is **preplanned coordination + leader-level voice control**.

## 8. 1940-41 warning/readiness system compounds the effect

Existing worldline authority closes the primary 1940-41 warning improvement as:
- observer posts;
- direct telephone/radio reporting;
- plotting rooms;
- standardized report format;
- readiness states.

This is exactly the institutional infrastructure that converts better fighter radios into operational value.

The control chain becomes:

detect
-> standardized report
-> plot
-> classify broad threat
-> choose fighter element
-> alert/launch
-> broad vector
-> CAP leader visual acquisition
-> leader-level engagement
-> status report / relief / reform.

The controller does not need individual-fighter radar tracks for this to work.

## 9. 1941 carrier-force fighter control — working state

By late 1941, central first-line carrier fighter practice should include:

### Pre-contact
- assigned CAP sector / altitude band;
- designated CAP leader;
- relief timing;
- deck-ready element;
- common threat-bearing convention;
- standard contact report fields.

### On contact
CAP leader can report:
- contact bearing;
- rough altitude;
- rough strength/type;
- engaged / not engaged;
- request reinforcement.

Controller can order at group level:
- continue sector;
- shift bearing;
- retain high cover;
- reinforce;
- return/refuel;
- launch ready section.

### After merge
Control degrades.

The ship usually does not know:
- exact individual fighter position;
- exact energy;
- exact fuel;
- exact target.

But it may still know:
- Shotai A engaged north;
- Shotai B remains available/high;
- leader C reports low fuel;
if those reports get through.

This is a material difference from "fighters are essentially on their own once launched."

## 10. December 1941-April 1942 — combat feedback accelerates refinement

War against the U.S./British creates new problems:
- stronger defensive formations;
- carrier radar;
- more durable aircraft;
- multi-altitude attacks;
- heavier AA;
- new aircraft types.

The Japanese do not need years to learn every tactical detail.

Working adaptation through spring 1942:
- more explicit target-priority calls;
- more emphasis on retaining at least one uncommitted/high element when threat picture is incomplete;
- reports of low-level torpedo threat versus high dive-bomber threat;
- tighter fuel/relief reporting;
- prebriefed chase limits / carrier-defense boundary, though aggressive pilots may violate them;
- repeated after-action changes to approach direction and firing geometry.

This is bounded by:
- elite-pilot individual initiative;
- offensive culture;
- uneven unit adoption;
- combat-channel congestion;
- incomplete threat picture.

## 11. Historical sanity anchors

Historical Japan demonstrates institutional learning even without this world's communication improvement:
- China experience influenced carrier concentration and the late-1940 carrier "box" concept;
- U.S. observers after Midway explicitly noted Japanese fighter attack methods had changed from Coral Sea;
- the historical Midway CAP problem was aggravated by unreliable fighter radios and lack of real shipboard fighter direction.

Therefore it is implausible for this worldline to gain reliable radio installation years earlier, fight continuously, and still preserve the full historical communications-doctrine bottleneck unchanged.

## 12. 1942 spring doctrinal state — revised center

Use this as the current working center for IJN first-line carrier fighters:

### Unit structure
- formal basic fighter unit remains three-plane shotai;
- chutai remains the larger tactical grouping;
- no universal two-plane conversion.

### Intra-shotai
- leader-centered radio control;
- wider/flexible combat spacing;
- split/reform calls;
- attack/support/lookout roles can be assigned;
- sequential passes better coordinated.

### Chutai
- shotai target/role allocation;
- one shotai may be retained as high cover/local reserve;
- leader reports group state.

### Carrier/division control
- sector and altitude-band assignments;
- ready/relief schedule;
- broad ship-to-CAP vectors;
- leader-level retask;
- cross-carrier prebriefed coordination where the division is operating together.

### What remains absent
- precision radar altitude;
- continuous individual tracking;
- automatic IFF;
- modern CIC;
- U.S./RAF-style mature GCI;
- reliable fleet-wide common air picture.

## 13. New control-state semantics

Replace a simple "radio works / does not work" model with two axes.

### COM — communication quality
COM0:
- no useful voice.

COM1:
- intermittent/simple calls.

COM2:
- reliable leader-level voice for ordinary tactical calls.

COM3:
- good local tactical voice / low congestion for a limited period.

Worldline first-line A6M:
- COM2 is a normal planning state when equipment is serviceable;
- COM3 can occur locally;
- COM0/1 remain possible from damage, propagation, installation fault or congestion.

### CMD — procedural control maturity
CMD0:
- visual-only / individual initiative.

CMD1:
- shotai leader control.

CMD2:
- chutai / multiple-shotai task allocation and reform procedures.

CMD3:
- carrier/base controller can maintain useful group-level sector/task picture and issue broad retask.

By spring 1942 first-line IJN carrier aviation:
- CMD2 should be normal;
- CMD3 is achievable pre-contact / early-contact when warning/plot/reporting are good;
- CMD3 degrades toward CMD2/CMD1 during a dense merge.

No CMD4 precision GCI is created.

## 14. Relation to Type21

Type21 does not create the doctrine.

The doctrine already exists because:
- radio reliability;
- war experience;
- plotting/reporting/readiness procedures.

Type21 adds a new sensor input to that existing control loop.

Thus Type21's spring-1942 value is larger than in a force that has never learned how to:
- standardize reports;
- designate sectors;
- launch a reserve;
- talk to CAP leaders.

But its technical limits remain:
- coarse bearing;
- weak altitude information;
- no continuous individual tracking.
