## 1. Core finding

The thick branch matters only if it attacks **multiple U.S. operational bottlenecks at once**.

A larger Japanese reinforcement does not become strategically transformative merely because:
- more Japanese troops survive;
- U.S. casualties rise;
- Saipan takes longer.

To fracture FORAGER, it must simultaneously stress:

1. **ground formations**
   - 2d Marine Division
   - 4th Marine Division
   - 27th Infantry Division
   - command cadre / artillery / engineers / replacements;

2. **amphibious reuse**
   - landing craft / LVTs;
   - boat crews;
   - beach-control / shore-party systems;
   - transports / LST / LCI turnaround;

3. **naval support**
   - old-BB / cruiser / DD NGFS;
   - CVE direct support;
   - ammunition / fuel / repair;
   - screening / ASW / SAR;

4. **fast-fleet operational freedom**
   - TF58 deck cycles;
   - carrier / escort damage;
   - DD allocation;
   - fuel / replenishment;
   - next-search / next-strike freedom.

If only one layer is stressed, the U.S. can absorb the cost.
If all four remain tied to Saipan, the sequential campaign starts to fracture.

## 2. Why FORAGER is structurally vulnerable to this

Historical FORAGER was sequential and reused assets.

Basic structure:
- Saipan first;
- same northern assault forces then reused for Tinian;
- Guam planned only days later with a separate southern assault force sharing naval / air support.

Historical reality already broke that schedule:
- Guam was postponed by over a month;
- Saipan resistance forced the 27th Division reserve into battle;
- the same 2d / 4th Marine Divisions later had to regroup for Tinian.

Therefore Branch does not need to invent a fragile U.S. plan.
The plan was already highly coupled.

## 3. Thick reinforcement threshold

Current fallback DD7 + Nisshin:
- ~490–690 personnel;
- ~280–440 t useful Saipan cargo.

Effect:
- prolong denial;
- increase casualties;
- preserve artillery / signals / repair;
- does **not** create a new maneuver mass.

Thick branch target:
**~2,000–3,500 personnel class**
plus materially larger heavy / specialist cargo.

Operational meaning:
- fresh battalion / brigade-slice reserve;
- replacement artillery / signal / engineer capacity;
- enough personnel to relieve exhausted defenders;
- veteran garrison cells become available for renewed local offense.

This is the first reinforcement scale that can plausibly create a **tactical phase reversal**, not just delay.

## 4. The desired 20/21-Jun operation is not merely “relief”

Reframe as a combined Army-Navy operation with four simultaneous effects.

### A. Fleet pressure
- carrier system prevents U.S. total air freedom;
- battle line prevents Lee from freely crushing the transport force;
- slow BB / CA bombardment disrupts U.S. shore support.

### B. Fast reinforcement
- multiple fast transport groups;
- Nisshin + one seaplane tender candidate;
- DD / cruiser / minelayer / landing-ship lift;
- cross-loaded personnel / cargo.

### C. Ground phase reversal attempt
Fresh arrivals first:
- backfill positions;
- restore signals / artillery / engineers;
- create a reserve.

Existing terrain-experienced defenders then:
- conduct the local counterattack / infiltration;
- exploit known routes / target boxes;
- avoid wasting fresh arrivals in immediate frontal assault.

### D. Air / island denial
- Tinian / Guam / water-air contact;
- bounded night harassment;
- artillery spotting / relay;
- morning air defense / local strike if physically supportable.

The operation therefore becomes:
**temporary local sea/air denial -> brigade-scale reinforcement -> ground-system reconstitution -> renewed operational denial.**

## 5. What would fracture the U.S. ground plan

The U.S. problem is not merely KIA/WIA totals.

Critical effects include:
- rifle company depletion;
- loss of company / battalion leadership;
- artillery / FDC / engineer specialist casualties;
- tank / vehicle loss;
- fatigue;
- inability to rotate battalions out of line;
- reserve formations already committed.

Historical central Saipan alone produced severe command-cadre losses in the 27th Division.

Branch thick-reinforcement objective:
force the U.S. to use the formations intended for:
- consolidation;
- Tinian preparation;
- reserve;
for continued Saipan clearance instead.

The key gate is:
**2d / 4th Marine + 27th Division cannot simultaneously finish Saipan and prepare the next assault.**

Once true, Tinian becomes an independent future operation rather than a seamless follow-on.

## 6. What would fracture the amphibious plan

Even if enough infantry replacements exist, the project can still stall through the assault system.

Track:
- LVT physical / MR;
- LCM / LCVP / LCI / LST damage and maintenance;
- boat-crew fatigue;
- shore-party losses;
- beach-control personnel;
- repair / tow demand;
- transport casualty evacuation burden.

A Saipan fight that continues to consume:
- tractors;
- boats;
- landing ships;
- medical lift;
- engineer equipment
prevents easy immediate conversion of the same amphibious system toward Tinian / Guam.

This is the important difference between:
“more casualties”
and
“the invasion project loses throughput.”

## 8. The decisive second-order effect: Japan gets another clock

This is the strategic payoff the thin relief cannot create by itself.

If U.S. FORAGER remains locked to Saipan for several additional days:

Japan may gain time to:
- withdraw the first intervention force;
- refuel DD / cruiser / carrier groups;
- repair light / medium damage;
- reorganize air groups;
- absorb surviving Marianas / second-echelon aircraft and crews;
- restore torpedo / ammunition loads;
- rebuild search / contact plans.

The Japanese fleet does **not** return fresh.

But it may recover from:
**spent / damaged / withdrawing**
to
**capable of another bounded intervention.**

That produces a recursive problem for the U.S.:

> finishing Saipan requires the fleet to stay;
> the fleet staying gives Japan time to regenerate;
> Japanese regeneration threatens another intervention;
> threat of intervention keeps more U.S. fleet power tied to Saipan.

This feedback loop is the first path to a true operational crack.

## 9. Re-engagement threshold

Do not promise a second decisive battle automatically.

A second Japanese intervention becomes plausible only if:

1. main carrier hulls survive;
2. carrier air can regenerate a useful strike / CAP nucleus;
3. oilers / fuel route survive;
4. modern DD screen remains sufficient;
5. damaged surface ships have enough mobility;
6. U.S. remains tied to Saipan rather than withdrawing east;
7. search / contact produces a usable opportunity.

Candidate second-use classes:
- renewed carrier harassment / strike;
- second surface pressure pulse;
- another bounded reinforcement;
- attack on U.S. support / replenishment;
- forcing TF58 / CVE dispersal.

Not necessarily:
- a second full-scale Philippine-Sea-style carrier duel.

## 10. Project-fracture ladder

Use the following phase labels.

### P0 — historical-style sequential campaign
Saipan -> regroup -> Tinian / Guam.

### P1 — delay / attrition
Saipan takes longer and costs more.
Follow-ons still proceed after schedule slip.

### P2 — follow-on break
Tinian / Guam cannot launch on the existing force / support cycle.
Ground formations and amphibious support require real regeneration / reinforcement.

### P3 — FORAGER operational lock
Saipan still consumes:
- divisions;
- CVE / NGFS;
- shipping / landing craft;
- Fifth Fleet screening / logistics
while Japan retains enough fleet capability to threaten another intervention.

### P4 — campaign redesign
U.S. must:
- pause;
- bring new formations / amphibious assets / replacement personnel;
- alter objective sequence;
- or terminate immediate Marianas follow-on operations.

The thick counterlanding branch is the first current scenario mechanism with a credible path from P1/P2 toward **P3**.

P4 remains a high bar and should not be selected without quantitative U.S. force / shipping / casualty audit.

## 11. What “Japan wins” means here

Not:
- destroy U.S. fleet;
- throw Marines into sea;
- permanently hold Saipan.

Operational Japanese success can instead be:

- Saipan remains denied long enough to consume the U.S. invasion reserve;
- Tinian / Guam sequencing collapses;
- U.S. amphibious formations require refit / replacements;
- Fifth Fleet support remains tied / depleted;
- Japanese fleet survives enough to reappear;
- the next U.S. operation begins from a damaged, delayed and less predictable baseline.

This creates strategic value even if Saipan eventually falls.
