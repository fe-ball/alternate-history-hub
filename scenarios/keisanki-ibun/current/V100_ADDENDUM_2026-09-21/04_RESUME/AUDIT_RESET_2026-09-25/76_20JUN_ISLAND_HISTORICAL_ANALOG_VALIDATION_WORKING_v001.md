## 1. Direct historical Saipan check — strongest evidence

### 15 Jun night
NHHC's Saipan history records:
- landings continued into the night;
- U.S. ships were firing over the beaches;
- **Japanese aircraft dropped flares** over the fighting area.

This directly validates:
- Japanese night-air illumination / local marking around the beachhead;
- an active island / air / naval night environment rather than a clean surface-only battle.

### 19 Jun ~01:00
NHHC H-Gram 032 records:
- a Japanese aircraft, probably from Guam, dropped **float flares near TG 58.1**;
- this was interpreted as the possible precursor to a night torpedo attack;
- no attack actually followed.

This validates:
- Guam / Marianas night aircraft can reach the carrier / support operating area;
- flare / reconnaissance action does not automatically imply an attack;
- illumination / contact support can be useful even with zero subsequent hit.

### Night of 20 Jun — exact-date calibration
U.S. Navy contemporary communique for 20 Jun reports:
- **several Japanese aircraft dropped bombs near transports and along shore**;
- no damage was reported;
- **sporadic Japanese shore-battery fire was directed at U.S. ships**;
- exposed emplacements were quickly countered / knocked out.

This is almost exactly the mission class represented in file 75:
- small night harassment, not a large strike;
- 0 major direct ship hit center;
- shore batteries active but exposed batteries pay a rapid counterbattery cost.

Therefore the file-75 center of:
- 3–5 night attack sorties;
- 0 major ship hit;
- a short interruption / maneuver effect;
is historically conservative/plausible, not merely explanatory bookkeeping.

## 2. Tinian shore-battery check

Historical Tinian, 24 Jul 1944:
- concealed Japanese 6-in battery survived prolonged bombardment;
- USS Colorado was hit **22 times** in a few minutes at roughly 3,000 yd;
- USS Norman Scott was also hit repeatedly;
- the battery was then suppressed/destroyed by return fire.

This validates the qualitative rule:
**a concealed island battery that survives suppression can be highly dangerous when ships close.**

But it also validates the counter-rule:
**once exposed, a battery under concentrated U.S. naval counterfire has a short life unless it can displace / alternate positions.**

Current Saipan model therefore remains appropriate:
- coast batteries are short-window local naval factors;
- they do not dominate 15–20 nmi outer combat;
- U.S. counterbattery progressively suppresses exposed cells.

### Tinian route correction
File 73 was deliberately conservative and gave the Tinian DD pair no free shore-battery kill.

Historical evidence supports upgrading Tinian from:
- “airfield / receiving node only”
to
- **“airfield + receiving + local coastal observation/fire threat”**.

Selected correction:
- U.S. DDs pressing close to the Tinian receiving axis must respect possible concealed shore batteries;
- this can push them seaward / shorten a close pursuit;
- **no additional U.S. DD hit is booked** because exact 20-Jun battery location / arc / readiness remains unclosed.

This strengthens the route geometry without inventing a kill.

## 4. Guadalcanal / Tokyo Express — reinforcement architecture

### Dark-moon timing
NHHC Cape Esperance account notes:
- Tokyo Express runs were deliberately timed around the **dark phase of the moon**.

This strongly supports the current decision to use the 20/21-Jun new-moon darkness for:
- transport penetration;
- multiple routes;
- brief unloading windows;
without treating darkness as radar immunity.

### Nisshin / Chitose heavy reinforcement
11/12 Oct 1942:
- Nisshin + Chitose acted as heavy reinforcement transports;
- six destroyers accompanied them;
- cranes were specifically useful for heavy artillery / equipment;
- a separate bombardment force covered the operation;
- scout aircraft detected them but misclassified the tenders as cruisers;
- Japanese fighter cover was considered important enough that the final Zeros remained until after nightfall and ditched when fuel was exhausted.

This directly validates the mission architecture:
- fast DD layer;
- large tender/heavy transport behind it;
- separate gun cover;
- imperfect enemy classification;
- aviation support expended for the transport mission.

Current file-71/73 architecture is therefore historically well grounded.

## 5. Tassafaronga — radar first contact does not settle the mission

30 Nov 1942:
- U.S. force had SG radar;
- detected the Japanese first at long range;
- opened fire first;
- Japanese destroyers were configured for supply transport, including removal of some torpedo reload burden;
- Type 93 reaction caused catastrophic U.S. cruiser losses.

But:
- the Japanese supply mission itself did not achieve its intended delivery.

This is an especially useful analogue because it separates:
- tactical naval damage
from
- transport mission success.

Current 20/21-Jun model does the same:
- U.S. SG first-contact advantage remains;
- Japanese Type 93 can still create an interruption window;
- success is measured by useful cargo/personnel ashore, not by cruiser kills.

The current result — one U.S. DD total loss but U.S. sea-control system intact, with partial Japanese delivery — is qualitatively compatible with this kind of trade.

## 6. Japanese floatplane / patrol-plane support of night reinforcement

Late Guadalcanal / Operation KE provides a direct analogue.

NHHC records:
- Japanese floatplanes / patrol planes covered destroyer reinforcement / evacuation runs;
- at night they could detect PT-boat wakes;
- air cover helped drive PT boats off;
- in one Operation KE action a Japanese seaplane destroyed PT-123.

This is the strongest historical validation for file 75's:
- **1–2 materially relevant water-air night sorties**
for:
- recontact;
- illumination;
- relay;
- local anti-small-craft / corridor support.

It also shows why the effect should be:
- local and tactical;
- particularly relevant against small surface craft;
- not a universal night search grid.

Therefore 1–2 sorties is conservative and should remain.

## 7. U.S. small-craft / radar defense analogue

Guadalcanal also demonstrates the other side.

U.S. PT boats:
- were dispatched when Japanese reinforcement runs were reported by aircraft / coastwatchers;
- deployed to likely channels around Savo;
- sometimes inflicted torpedo damage;
- were themselves vulnerable to Japanese destroyer gunfire, shore batteries and floatplanes.

This validates the current structure in which:
- U.S. interception uses fixed likely approach corridors;
- island / aircraft reports feed small-ship positioning;
- the defender is not omniscient;
- the attacker can use a different route or torpedo threat to force displacement.

## 8. Reverse-slope / surviving-defense analogue

Saipan, Guam and Peleliu all show that:
- preparatory naval/air bombardment could destroy many exposed positions;
- concealed / underground / reverse-slope defenses could survive;
- surviving positions remained capable of serious observed fire;
- exposed firing positions then attracted intense naval/artillery suppression.

Therefore the Branch island model:
- alternate OPs;
- reverse-slope mortar/artillery;
- short exposure cycles;
- pre-registered boxes;
- no “all shore defenses destroyed” assumption
has a strong historical basis.

The Branch improvement is not inventing the mechanism.
It is giving the mechanism better survey / target-number / communications persistence.

## 9. What should NOT be added

Historical validation does **not** support:

- a large 20/21-Jun local night strike;
- continuous airborne spotting over Saipan all night;
- Shiun automatically operating at night;
- coast batteries producing regular long-range hits on maneuvering ships;
- Tinian shore batteries automatically sinking one of the U.S. DDs;
- Guam launching another independent close-fight package;
- aircraft and coast artillery creating radar blindness.

Keep:
- small night harassment;
- sparse water-air support;
- local shore-fire danger;
- rapid U.S. counterbattery;
- incomplete / intermittent track.
