# 0. Historical answer

**YES. Tinian was an active cross-channel fire threat during the Saipan campaign.**

This is not only a late 6/7-Jul nuisance event.

Historical sequence:

- U.S. naval gunfire against Tinian began before the Saipan landing specifically to prevent Tinian guns and aircraft from interfering with the Saipan operation.
- 14 Jun:
  - Tinian shore batteries engaged U.S. ships supporting Saipan reconnaissance / bombardment.
- 15 Jun Saipan D-Day:
  - a Tinian 4.7-inch-class battery hit USS Tennessee three times;
  - Tennessee suffered 8 killed and 25 wounded while still supporting the Saipan landing.
- Destroyer patrols remained active in Saipan Channel to suppress Tinian batteries and Ushi Point.
- 20 Jun:
  - U.S. 155-mm Long Tom artillery on Saipan began firing directly on Tinian;
  - more Army/Marine artillery was subsequently added.
- 6/7 Jul:
  - Tinian shore batteries shelled Isely/Aslito;
  - U.S. destroyer and artillery counterfire quickly silenced them.

Therefore:
> **Tinian fire was a real tactical participant in the Saipan operation, but U.S. suppression prevented it from becoming a continuous free-fire system.**

# 1. Historical Tinian firepower

Historical Tinian possessed a substantial mixed Army/Navy artillery system.

Army:
- 12 x 75-mm mountain guns in the 50th Infantry artillery battalion;
- additional battalion infantry-gun / 70-mm support;
- mobile artillery concept tied to counterattack.

Navy fixed / dual-purpose:
- 10 x 140-mm coast-defense guns;
- 10 x 120-mm dual-purpose guns;
- 4 x 76.2-mm dual-purpose guns;
- 3 x British-origin 6-inch coast-defense guns;
- additional AA / automatic weapons.

Distribution included:
- Ushi Point;
- Faibus San Hilo Point;
- Asiga Bay;
- Gurguan Point;
- Tinian Harbor / Tinian Town.

The channel is narrow enough that a meaningful subset of these medium/heavy guns can physically influence:
- southern Saipan approaches;
- Saipan Channel;
- Agingan/Nafutan-side water;
- later Aslito/Isely and southern support areas,
subject to firing arc / emplacement geometry.

Do not assume every fixed gun can bear on every Saipan target.

---

# 2. Historical survivability lesson

The three cave-concealed 6-inch guns at Tinian Town:
- survived extensive pre-invasion neutralization;
- remained unknown until they fired on 24 Jul;
- heavily damaged Colorado and Norman Scott;
- were not fully eliminated immediately.

Therefore even historical Tinian already demonstrates:
**fixed battery concealment can convert “suppressed” into “temporarily quiet.”**

This is the physical basis for Branch nonlinearity.

---

# 3. What U.S. tactical behavior proves

U.S. behavior is itself evidence of the threat.

Before and during the Saipan landing:
- dedicated ships were assigned to Tinian suppression;
- destroyers patrolled Saipan Channel;
- Tinian airfields and batteries received repeated harassing/counterbattery fire.

After U.S. artillery came ashore:
- 155-mm guns were turned south against Tinian while Saipan fighting was still active.

This means:
> Tinian suppression was not a future Tinian-invasion preparation only.
>
> It was already a **Saipan-protection mission.**

# 6. Tactical employment during the Saipan battle

## D-Day / 15 Jun

Priority:
1. U.S. fire-support ships in southern Saipan geometry;
2. landing-craft / support-water boxes where visible;
3. Agingan / southern beachhead support;
4. identified artillery/vehicle concentrations if range permits.

Historical Tennessee hit proves this phase must exist.

Branch effect:
- more disciplined short missions;
- faster cease-fire after U.S. counterbattery;
- better chance some batteries survive for later use.

No sustained mass barrage.

---

## 16–19 Jun

As U.S. front moves inland:

Priority shifts toward:
- southern road movement;
- artillery parks;
- Aslito approaches;
- engineer / vehicle concentration;
- known fixed support zones.

Best firing windows:
- night;
- poor visibility;
- U.S. fleet-defense distraction;
- fresh Tinian/water-air observation.

U.S. response:
- DD counterbattery;
- CVE / air strike;
- naval gunfire;
- later Saipan artillery.

Main effect:
**mission-allocation and work interruption**, not terrain reversal.

---

## 20–25 Jun

From 20 Jun onward the U.S. historical/Branch ability to fire 155-mm from Saipan onto Tinian makes exposed Tinian batteries increasingly expensive to use.

Therefore:
- fire becomes shorter;
- reappearance after suppression becomes the Branch payoff;
- synchronized firing with:
  - Japanese naval relief;
  - local air;
  - Saipan artillery/counterattack
  becomes much more attractive than independent harassment.

This period should treat Tinian as a second fire-direction axis against the U.S. southern support system.

---

## Late Jun – 5/6 Jul

Best targets:
- Aslito repair / fuel / aircraft support;
- road/vehicle flow;
- southern artillery/support;
- dawn sortie cycle.

This is already partially represented in files116/119.

The correction is to treat this as the culmination of a continuing cross-channel system, not its sudden appearance.
