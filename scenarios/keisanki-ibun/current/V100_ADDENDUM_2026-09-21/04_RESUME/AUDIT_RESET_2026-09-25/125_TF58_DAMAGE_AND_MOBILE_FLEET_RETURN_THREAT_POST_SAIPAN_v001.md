# 7. Why this favors Tinian operationally

Tinian is uniquely attractive under fleet-return threat because the operation can sit under a growing **Saipan-based support umbrella**.

Advantages:

1. shore-to-shore distance;
2. Saipan artillery can support;
3. Aslito can provide increasing fighter/recon/tactical support;
4. CVEs/old BB can carry much of the direct-support bill;
5. TF58 fast carriers can remain farther west as fleet cover;
6. taking Tinian removes the principal surviving local Japanese air/observation node;
7. Tinian loss reduces Japanese ability to:
   - recontact U.S. carrier/amphibious groups;
   - receive carrier-origin aircraft;
   - support a returning mobile fleet.

Thus Tinian is not only a next island.

It is:
> **the operation that improves the geometry of the next fleet battle.**

# 8. Why this penalizes Guam-first

A Guam-first assault requires:

- a larger oceanic amphibious convoy;
- broader CAP/search/ASW;
- heavier carrier dependence;
- separation from Saipan shore artillery;
- transport exposure farther south/west.

If Tinian remains Japanese:
- Tinian can still provide recon/attack;
- Guam itself remains an aviation node;
- a Japanese mobile-fleet return can attack against a better local ISR network.

Therefore Guam-first creates the least attractive geometry for a damaged-but-powerful TF58:
> **large amphibious force exposed while two Japanese Marianas aviation nodes still exist and the mobile fleet remains alive.**

This does not make Guam impossible.
It raises the fleet-cover cost materially.
