## 1. Strategic objective must be rewritten correctly

The operation cannot rationally have:
**save Saipan permanently / restore full Japanese control**
as its primary success criterion.

The landed package is too small for that:
- Saipan useful landed: ~490–690 personnel;
- useful cargo: ~280–440 t;
- Tinian separately: ~160–240 personnel + ~50–90 t.

Against the scale of the island campaign, this is not a new army and not a general resupply pipeline.

The rational strategic objective is narrower:

1. delay U.S. conversion of Aslito / Saipan into a secure operational base;
2. preserve Japanese artillery / observation / engineering / signals / repair function for another useful interval;
3. force U.S. naval / air / artillery tasking to remain tied to Saipan;
4. inflict disproportionate short-horizon losses while Japanese fleet concentration still exists;
5. preserve the main carrier / battle fleet for later operations.

Therefore measure success in:
**time, U.S. tasking tax, denial persistence and specialist-function survival**,
not territory restored or total tonnage delivered.

---

## 2. Why support is strategically worth considering

Saipan is not an ordinary isolated garrison.

Its loss:
- breaches the inner defensive perimeter;
- threatens the Marianas air system;
- enables eventual B-29 operations against the Home Islands;
- worsens sea / air geometry around the western Pacific.

Historically the Marianas breach was considered serious enough to trigger A-Go and the sought decisive fleet action.

Therefore complete fleet passivity while a physically supportable intervention window exists would also carry a real strategic cost.

The question is not:
“why risk ships for an island?”

It is:
“how much fleet risk is justified to delay the strategic conversion of the Marianas?”

## 3. What the current operation does well

### A. It avoids the worst Japanese historical pattern: feeding the garrison by attritional repetition

The current operation is a **single concentrated intervention**, not a promise of nightly Tokyo-Express repetition.

This matters.

Repeated DD / tender runs under 1944 U.S. air and radar dominance would:
- consume the modern-DD force;
- consume fuel;
- create predictable routes;
- eventually lose Nisshin / cruisers;
- weaken carrier / fleet screens.

Current support is strategically acceptable only if treated as:
**one high-leverage pulse, followed by withdrawal / reassessment.**

### B. It uses lower-opportunity-cost battleships for shore fire

Fuso / Yamashiro / Ise / Hyuga:
- are less valuable than the fast / heavy line for a future high-speed fleet decision;
- can convert their 35.6-cm batteries and surviving secondaries into immediate ground effect.

This is a good allocation.

The damaged Fuso-class ship is not forced into the close-secondary lane.

### C. It does NOT spend Yamato / Musashi / Nagato / Mutsu as generic bombardment batteries

Heavy line role remains:
- Lee blocking;
- counter-interdiction;
- fleet-battle threat.

Their shore-fire contribution is short / bounded.

This is strategically important.
The operation does not trade the main heavy fleet's battle role for a few more shore shells.

### D. Kongo / CA layer is used as corridor cover rather than sacrificed into a decisive duel

Fast layer:
- creates surface cover;
- fires short pulses;
- maneuvers;
- does not chase Lee.

Again, mission discipline is good.

### E. DDs are used for the thing only DDs can do

The relief DDs:
- penetrate;
- use Type 93 to break pursuit;
- carry compact high-value cargo;
- exploit multiple routes.

This is a better strategic use than treating them as generic escorts during the one period when speed / torpedo threat can create a delivery window.

But the count is near the upper acceptable limit.

### F. Nisshin is released conditionally, not by schedule

This is the most important good decision.

Nisshin only commits after:
- D1 disrupts the U.S. screen;
- D2/D3 prove a corridor;
- current reports show Lee has not cut the route.

Thus the large transport is not risked simply because the operation plan says “go.”

This converts Nisshin from a sacrificial transport into a **contingent exploitation asset**.

### G. Carrier aviation is preserved

The operation does not use valuable carrier aircraft as a close night-support arm.

Island aviation / water-air performs:
- small harassment;
- contact;
- relay;
- illumination;
- SAR.

That is a much better use of scarce night-capable crews.

## 4. Where the current operation is strategically dangerous

### A. The surface fleet concentration is expensive in fuel

The fast-vanguard system had already been near F-AMBER-RED in the earlier fuel ledger.
Destroyers are especially sensitive.

A 20/21-Jun high-speed surface intervention:
- spends fuel recovered by replenishment / rotation;
- requires another safe withdrawal / replenishment cycle;
- increases dependence on vulnerable oilers;
- reduces freedom to choose the next operation.

Therefore the relief cannot become habitual.

### B. Seven relief-convertible DDs + two Nisshin escorts is a large modern-DD commitment

If losses were worse by only a few hulls:
- carrier screens;
- heavy-line screens;
- oiler protection;
- future torpedo force
would all suffer.

The selected result is tolerable.
The architecture itself is not cheap.

### C. Nisshin's delivered tonnage is strategically useful but not transformative

~130–210 t useful heavy/bulk cargo from Nisshin is valuable if it is:
- artillery / mortar ammunition;
- communications;
- engineering;
- repair;
- medical;
- vehicle parts.

It is poor value if used as:
- ordinary food;
- generic rifle ammunition;
- mass infantry lift.

Thus the selected cargo prioritization is mandatory to the strategic logic.

### D. The operation creates a dawn-risk tail

By ~23:45:
- one transport DD is only ~18–22 kt;
- other DDs are damaged;
- Nisshin has been identified as a high-value large contact;
- U.S. sea control remains intact.

The operation is not strategically closed until these ships clear the next daylight search / strike envelope.

A successful unloading followed by daylight destruction of Nisshin + multiple DDs could turn the exchange negative.

## 5. Alternative-course comparison

### Option A — no major surface support
Use:
- island defense;
- small craft;
- submarines;
- limited air / water-air;
- conserve the fleet.

Advantage:
- minimum fleet risk / fuel cost.

Disadvantage:
- U.S. support system recovers normally;
- Aslito security / base conversion accelerates;
- Japanese island artillery / communications / engineering degrade without a large replenishment pulse;
- no forced Lee / CA/DD tasking.

Assessment:
**too passive while a unique fleet-concentration window exists.**

### Option B — DD-only relief
Use:
- 5–7 DD;
- no Nisshin;
- no large bombardment concentration.

Advantage:
- lower risk;
- fast compact cargo / specialists can still arrive.

Disadvantage:
- less heavy ammunition / engineering / repair mass;
- weaker ground-support shock;
- U.S. fire-support system remains more coherent;
- DDs themselves face a harder reception.

Assessment:
**the safest credible alternative and probably preferable if the ground system is already near collapse.**

In the current Branch ground state, however, the island still has enough artillery / observation / repair structure for heavy cargo to produce real marginal value.
Therefore DD-only is not clearly superior.

### Option C — current bounded combined intervention
Use:
- queued shore bombardment;
- DD7;
- conditional Nisshin;
- fast / heavy surface cover;
- small island night-air / water-air support;
- local ground exploitation;
- cut off before midnight.

Advantage:
- concentrates multiple partial advantages into one short window;
- heavy cargo can actually be converted into surviving ground fire / repair;
- U.S. tasking is split across land / sea / air;
- preserves the carrier arm.

Disadvantage:
- substantial fuel / escort / hull-risk bill;
- requires disciplined termination.

Assessment:
**best fit to the current Branch state, but only as a one-shot intervention.**

### Option D — decisive night fleet battle to “save” the relief
Use:
- Lee engagement as main objective;
- keep Nisshin unloading while heavy fleets fight.

Advantage:
- potentially larger naval result.

Disadvantage:
- transforms a bounded support mission into a general fleet gamble;
- gives U.S. radar fire control more sustained exposure time;
- risks the heavy fleet for a garrison that still cannot be permanently resupplied.

Assessment:
**strategically unsound.**

## 6. Missing optimization — make the lift two-way

The current support concept is still too one-directional.

After unloading, returning DD / Nisshin capacity should, where station time permits, prioritize selective evacuation of:
- seriously wounded who can actually be moved;
- recovered / stranded aircrew;
- scarce radio / radar / fire-control / aviation technicians;
- high-value staff / intelligence / technical documents;
- personnel no longer useful in the island's remaining defense but valuable elsewhere.

Do NOT delay the withdrawal materially to maximize evacuation.

Reason:
if Saipan ultimately falls, a return leg that carries nothing wastes strategic value.
A limited two-way lift makes the same dangerous corridor serve both:
- immediate island denial,
and
- preservation of scarce Imperial human capital.

This is the principal strategic correction recommended by this audit.

Exact evacuee numbers remain OPEN until the post-unloading boat / station-time clock is checked.

## 7. Required stop conditions

The operation remains rational only if the following stop rules are enforced:

1. Nisshin does not enter without a proven corridor.
2. Nisshin leaves once Lee / SG pressure makes continued transfer require a general fleet battle.
3. Heavy line does not pursue Lee after the delivery objective is achieved.
4. Slow damaged DDs are not rescued at the cost of exposing the entire fleet.
5. No automatic repeat relief on 21/22 Jun.
6. Repeat intervention requires a new calculation of:
   - ground marginal value;
   - fuel;
   - DD screen depletion;
   - U.S. air-search / daylight risk;
   - remaining transport / receiving capacity.

Without these rules, the same operation shifts from rational to strategically wasteful very quickly.

## 8. Strategic judgment

Selected judgment:

**CONDITIONALLY SUITABLE — ONE-SHOT INTERVENTION, NOT SUSTAINMENT DOCTRINE.**

The operation is not justified by:
“support is always good”
or
“any U.S. suppression is worth something.”

It is justified because this specific Branch state combines:

- a strategically vital island still possessing a functioning denial system;
- a temporary concentration of Japanese surface power;
- a new-moon night;
- surviving local observation / artillery;
- a heavy transport physically capable of delivering the kinds of stores the defense still converts into combat value;
- a U.S. defender whose sea control is strong but must protect Saipan rather than freely chase west;
- Japanese ability to terminate the operation before it becomes a general fleet battle.

The operation is **not** a method for saving Saipan indefinitely.

It is a method for buying:
**denial time, U.S. tasking, ground-system persistence and attrition**
at a price the fleet can tolerate once.

The same price paid repeatedly would not be tolerable.
