## 0. Core decision

At 14:50 the U.S. has a real Japanese carrier-core contact from 13:05–13:20, but not a rational normal carrier-strike solution.

Selected decision:
- NO deep anti-carrier strike against the Japanese first-line carrier core;
- launch a fighter-heavy Marianas suppression/recovery-interdiction package against Tinian/Guam instead;
- continue western reconnaissance and retain Saipan-cover geometry.

This is not risk aversion by fiat. It follows from range, source age, daylight/recovery, and deck-task competition.

## 1. Why the Japanese carrier target remains RED

At the 13:05–13:20 U.S. contact the Japanese carrier core is about 590–620 nm away and already opening.

By 14:50:
- source age is roughly 90–105 minutes;
- Japanese carriers have continued WSW toward support/replenishment geometry;
- practical range is now roughly **630–670 nm class** in the selected sensitivity.

A normal coherent strike launched after 15:15 would need roughly 3+ hours outbound even before search/terminal maneuver, then an equally impossible carrier return in darkness/low fuel. The U.S. has no forward friendly recovery field west of the target.

Therefore:
- CONTACT = GREEN but ageing;
- POSITION = AMBER/GREEN for broad search;
- normal STRIKE RANGE = RED;
- ultra-long lightened strike remains physically imaginable but is not selected.

## 2. Why Marianas is the rational target

U.S. observation from the Japanese attack gives strong operational clues without omniscience:
- a western carrier-launched attack has just arrived;
- survivors are seen/expected to egress toward the Marianas;
- a smaller Marianas axis also attacked from the east/southeast;
- Guam/Tinian have already functioned as Japanese recovery/rearm nodes.

The U.S. does not know exact Japanese landing counts or maintenance queues.

But it can rationally infer:
- Tinian is a major immediate recovery node;
- Guam remains useful despite earlier damage;
- attacking the fixed recovery system can destroy/disable aircraft and delay regeneration without locating the distant carrier core.

## 3. U.S. deck regeneration after the Japanese strike

The Japanese attack ends around 14:40.

Selected recovery:
- affected light-carrier deck remains closed 45–75 minutes;
- other task-group decks resume orderly cycles progressively from about 15:05–15:30;
- TG58.4's relatively fresh decks materially support CAP recovery and new launch generation;
- F6F that just fought the Japanese raid are not instantly reused without refuel/rearm.

By roughly 15:30–15:45 the system can generate a bounded fighter-heavy package without stripping fleet defense.

## 4. Selected U.S. suppression package

Launch about **15:35–15:55**.

Total: **72–82 aircraft**
- F6F: **48–54**

- TBM: **10–12**

Primary effort:
- Tinian, because the recent eastern Japanese axis and western shuttle survivors make it the stronger inferred recovery node.

Secondary effort:

Selected split:
- Tinian: roughly 42–48 aircraft;
- Guam: roughly 28–34 aircraft.

No CVE close-support aircraft are added to this package.

Arrival:
- approximately **16:25–16:45** depending on island/route/assembly.

## 5. Japanese Marianas state before the raid

The 34–43 aircraft attempting/achieving Marianas recovery from the Japanese second strike begin arriving roughly 15:00–15:40.

They are not all MR:
- many are low fuel;
- some are damaged;
- crews are fatigued;
- ordnance/servicing lines are already burdened.

Radar warning and dispersal procedure remain active.

Selected immediate defense, charged to surviving local fighters and recovered fighter survivors:
- Tinian materially engaged fighters: **14–18**
- Guam materially engaged fighters: **10–14**
- total unique Japanese fighters materially engaged: **24–32**

This does NOT define total Marianas fighter inventory.

Some returning carrier-origin fighters are too low-fuel/damaged to count as fresh interceptors.

## 6. Radar warning and aircraft dispersion

Japanese base radar/plot detects the approaching U.S. package early enough to:
- launch selected ready fighters;
- halt/sequence landings;
- tow/disperse aircraft already on the ground;
- divert selected incoming aircraft toward Rota/other usable nodes when fuel permits;
- protect the most vulnerable servicing clusters.

It does not:
- identify exact U.S. aircraft count before visual confirmation;
- erase the attack;
- make every landed Japanese aircraft mobile.

Selected effect:
- **8–14** incoming/recovered Japanese aircraft are delayed, waved onward or diverted rather than being caught in a single landing/servicing cluster.
- diversion consumes fuel and increases later servicing friction.

## 7. Air battle

The U.S. package is deliberately fighter-heavy.

Japanese fighters use radar-directed sector placement and avoid prolonged equal-energy fights where possible, but they are tired and numerically inferior.

Selected combined Tinian/Guam result:
- Japanese airborne fighter irreversible: **7–11**
- Japanese additional damaged/short-unavailable: **5–8**
- U.S. F6F irreversible: **4–6**
- U.S. F6F additional damaged: **5–8**
- U.S. SB2C/TBM irreversible: **2–4**
- U.S. attack aircraft additional damaged/abort: **3–6**

U.S. retains local air superiority over the terminal attack period.

## 8. Ground/air-system effects

Because warning/dispersal works, the result is not a parked-aircraft massacre.

### Tinian

- local taxiway/apron/servicing restriction: **90–150 min**
- ground aircraft irreversible: **3–5**
- additional short-unavailable: **5–8**

### Guam
- normal runway/launch-recovery interruption: **25–50 min**
- local handling restriction: **60–120 min**
- ground aircraft irreversible: **2–3**
- additional short-unavailable: **3–5**

Combined:
- ground aircraft irreversible: **5–8**
- ground/servicing short-unavailable: **8–13**
- roughly **15–22 aircraft-class** of recovery/maintenance work is displaced or delayed by about **2–4 h**.

No centered catastrophic main fuel/ammunition-store destruction.
No centered mission-kill of the principal warning/plot network.
Water-air capability is not automatically silenced by runway cratering.

## 9. U.S. return

The suppression package begins returning roughly 17:15 onward.

At 17:30:
- part of the package is still airborne/approaching recovery;
- its aircraft cannot be counted as immediately available for another strike;
- deck recovery and damaged-aircraft handling again consume capacity.

Thus the afternoon suppression succeeds tactically but also consumes the same U.S. deck system needed for search, Saipan support, and any hypothetical anti-carrier strike.

## 13. Interpretation

The U.S. does not fail to attack the Japanese carriers because it lacks offensive strength. It lacks a rational range/recovery solution.

The fixed Marianas recovery system therefore becomes the U.S. operational substitute for a carrier strike.

Japanese air-ground separation remains valuable:
- many carrier-origin aircraft survive by landing forward;
- crews and airframes are not lost with a damaged host deck.

But the same system creates a vulnerability:
- repeated concentration at a small number of forward bases gives the U.S. fixed targets for suppression.

The Branch advantage is therefore real but not free.
