# 0. Core finding

Saipan U.S. loss must not be modeled as a uniform percentage removed from every unit.

The Branch creates repeated **functional targeting** of:
- artillery / FDC;
- observers;
- signals;
- engineers;
- vehicle/route-control;
- relief/handoff seams;
- battalion/regimental rear.

Therefore some U.S. units retain substantial raw headcount while losing the human structure that makes them reusable as formations.

The correct late-campaign picture includes many examples of:

> “enough men remain to hold ground, but the unit cannot be sent into another deliberate assault without reconstitution.”

and some examples of:

> “the battalion/company exists administratively, but for the next operational cycle it is effectively not a coherent combat formation.”

---

# 1. Four independent formation-health dimensions

Do not use one strength percentage.

For each battalion/regiment/support formation track:

## H — headcount / present-for-duty
How many bodies remain physically available.

## L — leadership skeleton
- battalion staff;
- company commanders;
- platoon leaders;
- first sergeants / platoon sergeants;
- squad leaders;
- experienced gun-section NCOs.

## S — specialist network
- FO/FDC;
- radio/wire;
- engineers;
- corpsmen/medical;
- weapons-company specialists;
- tank/vehicle crews;
- mechanics / recovery;
- route-control / shore-party specialists.

## P — psychological / fatigue coherence
- sleep;
- startle/freeze;
- concentration;
- confidence in exposed positions;
- willingness/ability to execute complex orders;
- cumulative combat exhaustion.

A unit can be:
- H 75 / L 45 / S 50 / P 40

and be much less useful than a unit at:
- H 65 / L 75 / S 80 / P 70.

---

# 2. Why Branch Saipan disproportionately hits L and S

Japanese targeting is not random.

Repeated mechanisms:
- registered artillery against gun/FDC/road boxes;
- observed fire against command/communications;
- night infiltration into rear/support areas;
- relief-seam attacks;
- final-operation penetration into V2 functions.

Thus:
- leaders expose themselves restoring broken lines;
- FOs / wire teams repeatedly reoccupy observable ground;
- engineers work on roads/obstacles under fire;
- vehicle and recovery crews repeatedly use fixed routes;
- medical/aid personnel accumulate near casualty flows.

Therefore the campaign produces a **leadership/specialist casualty concentration** above a uniform-manpower model.

Do not inflate total casualties to represent this.
Keep it as formation-quality damage.

---

# 3. Working late-campaign formation classes

Use four operational classes.

## F0 — coherent / reusable
Can conduct a deliberate attack after ordinary rest/ammunition replenishment.

## F1 — degraded but employable
Can:
- hold;
- patrol;
- conduct limited attack;
but has reduced depth / leadership / weapons coordination.

## F2 — reconstitution mandatory
Can still defend locally, but should not be treated as an intact maneuver battalion.

Needs:
- leader replacement;
- platoon/company recombination;
- weapons-company rebuilding;
- signals/FDC/medical restoration;
- 24–72+ h rest/reorganization.

## F3 — formation-broken
Administrative designation exists, but immediate coherent battalion employment is not credible.

Survivors may:
- hold a small perimeter;
- be attached piecemeal;
- form composite companies;
- be withdrawn and rebuilt.

This is not “annihilated.”
It is **temporarily dead as a maneuver formation.**

# 4. 5 Jul evening U.S. formation-health overlay

Across the 2d Marine Division, 4th Marine Division, 27th Infantry Division and attached local reinforcements:

### F0
**~8–11 infantry battalion-equivalents**

Includes:
- relatively fresh elements;
- U1/U2;
- units rotated earlier and successfully rebuilt;
- battalions with losses but intact command/specialist skeleton.

### F1
**~8–10 battalion-equivalents**

Still useful but visibly worn.

Typical state:
- H ~70–85%;
- L/S/P more degraded than H.

### F2
**~5–7 battalion-equivalents**

Require genuine reconstitution before another planned assault.

Typical causes:
- repeated frontline loss;
- command/NCO thinning;
- weapons-company/support attrition;
- exhaustion;
- too many replacements not yet integrated.

### F3
**~1–3 battalion-equivalents**

Not necessarily named whole battalions in every case.
This can also be:
- two broken battalions whose remnants form one composite battalion;
- several companies administratively present but not independently combat-usable.

Do not sum these as exact OOB without unit ledgers.
They are a formation-condition distribution.

# 5. What U1/U2 actually repair

Fresh battalions do more than add rifles.

They allow the U.S. to:

1. take a sector from an F2 unit;
2. remove that unit from continuous contact;
3. consolidate companies;
4. promote/redistribute leaders;
5. rebuild communications;
6. refill crew-served weapons;
7. redistribute corpsmen/engineers/FOs;
8. give men uninterrupted sleep;
9. identify severe psychiatric/exhaustion cases;
10. integrate replacements.

Therefore the real effect of U1/U2 is:
> **formation repair capacity.**

This is why Japanese interdiction of relief/handoff has leverage beyond the casualties inflicted during the relief itself.

---

# 6. Leader/NCO “つまみ食い” effect

Do not require absurd total officer casualties.

A battalion can become F2 if losses are concentrated in a few key nodes.

Illustrative formation mechanism:

- battalion CO/XO/staff mostly intact;
- but 2 of 3 rifle-company commanders replaced;
- several platoon leaders gone;
- multiple senior NCO vacancies;
- weapons-company section leaders lost;
- FO/radio teams repeatedly replaced.

Headcount may still look acceptable.

But:
- orders take longer;
- neighboring-unit coordination weakens;
- replacements cluster under inexperienced leaders;
- night control deteriorates;
- complex attacks are avoided;
- friendly-fire/no-fire friction rises.

Thus:
**leadership topology matters more than officer percentage alone.**

# 7. High-grade specialist attrition

The Branch creates repeated “small bites” from high-value personnel:

### Artillery/FDC
- FOs;
- fire-direction personnel;
- experienced gun chiefs;
- wire/radio.

### Engineer
- demolition specialists;
- route/bridge/obstacle teams;
- recovery personnel.

### Armor/vehicle
- tank commanders;
- experienced drivers;
- mechanics;
- recovery crews.

### Medical
- corpsmen;
- litter teams;
- forward aid staff.

### Signals
- battalion/regimental radio;
- wire teams;
- switchboard/control personnel.

The result is not just fewer specialists.

It creates:
- equipment sitting physically present but under-crewed;
- slower repair;
- more mistakes;
- longer handoff;
- higher burden on the surviving experts;
- further fatigue and psychiatric load.

This is a feedback loop.

# 8. Psychiatric / combat-exhaustion layer

Do not equate all combat exhaustion with permanent psychiatric injury.

Most acute cases can recover with:
- sleep;
- food;
- sedation/rest;
- removal from immediate combat;
- reassurance/reconditioning.

But Branch Saipan creates a subset of units with unusually severe concentration of symptoms because:
- repeated artillery exposure;
- interrupted sleep;
- several consecutive relief failures;
- night infiltration alarms;
- leader loss;
- repeated re-entry into exposed positions;
- seeing replacement leaders/crews hit again.

By late campaign some companies/battalions can contain conspicuous numbers of men showing what a modern clinician would recognize as severe acute combat-stress / trauma-spectrum symptoms.

Operational manifestations:
- tremor;
- mutism;
- freezing;
- inappropriate startle;
- panic episodes;
- dissociation-like states;
- confusion;
- inability to concentrate;
- inability to perform technical sequences;
- severe sleep disruption;
- autonomic vomiting/diarrhea;
- emotional flattening;
- reckless or mechanically compliant behavior without good judgment.

Use period administrative language:
**combat exhaustion / neuropsychiatric casualty**
rather than retroactively assigning one modern diagnosis to all cases.

---

# 9. Psychiatric burden by formation

Across the whole force, many cases are temporary.

But the important formation-level pattern is clustering.

### Normal-worn battalion
Recognized current exhaustion/NP cases:
**~1–3%**
plus a larger subclinical fatigue tail.

### Heavily hit F1/F2 battalion
Recognized current cases:
**~3–6%**
with perhaps another **~8–15%** performing below normal because of sleep debt, startle, concentration problems or emotional exhaustion.

### Worst repeatedly shelled / broken battalion or company group
Short-period recognized + obvious severe cases can reach:
**~6–10% class**
with a much larger degraded tail.

Do not apply 6–10% to the whole U.S. force.

The point is that some battered formations become clinically and operationally ugly even while the army-wide average remains manageable.

# 10. “重篤な戦時神経症人員だらけ” — correct interpretation

Yes, locally.

Not:
> every man in the battalion is psychiatrically incapacitated.

But plausibly:
- every platoon knows several men who have broken down;
- company HQ has multiple men under observation/rest;
- battalion aid station has recurring exhaustion cases;
- some technically uninjured men cannot be trusted with a complex night task;
- senior NCOs are spending time managing men who would normally require little supervision;
- replacements see veteran men visibly shaking/freezing/vomiting after bombardment.

This has a large morale and command effect beyond the formal NP casualty roll.

# 11. Final-operation shock on formation integrity

The 5/6 Jul operation hits a relief seam and V2 functions.

Therefore its ~1,150–1,550 gross casualties are not evenly distributed.

Immediate formation effect:

### One primary handoff battalion group
**F1/F2 -> F3 or deep F2**

### One adjacent/fresh relief element
**F0/F1 -> F1/F2**

### One artillery/FDC/support group
locally:
**function-broken for hours; personnel/equipment rebuilding required after recapture**

### Rear/security/medical/route personnel
new ad hoc casualties and exhaustion load.

Thus the final attack can create:
> **2–3 additional battalion-equivalent formations requiring serious reconstitution**
without killing 2–3 battalions outright.

# 12. 6–9 Jul U.S. “paper strength vs usable strength”

After the final operation, U.S. total surviving headcount remains overwhelming.

But usable formation inventory is temporarily much worse than paper OOB.

Working effect:

### Immediately after 6 Jul dawn
- F3: **~2–4 battalion-equivalents**
- F2: **~6–8**
- F1: **~7–9**
- F0: **~7–10**

Over 48–72 h:
- U1/U2 and intact support allow several F2/F3 units to be rebuilt;
- some F3 remnants are absorbed rather than restored as original battalions;
- serious leader/specialist vacancies remain.

The U.S. still wins because:
- total force is large;
- fire support survives;
- reserve exists;
- Japanese organized offensive power collapses.

But the cost is **formation debt** carried into the next operation.

---

# 13. Consequence for Tinian/Guam

This is the major strategic effect.

A unit can be:
- alive;
- not counted in J-DIRECT;
- administratively present;
yet still be unusable for the next amphibious assault on schedule.

Reasons:
- too many leaders replaced;
- weapons-company crews mixed;
- combat exhaustion;
- replacements not integrated;
- artillery/engineer specialist depletion;
- medical/transport recovery incomplete.

Therefore Saipan creates three separate debts:

1. **replacement demand** from J-DIRECT;
2. **medical/temporary absence** from C-OUT/current-unfit;
3. **formation-reconstitution debt** among survivors.

The third is why FORAGER schedule damage can exceed what the raw casualty count suggests.

---

# 14. Practical late-Saipan picture

By 8/9 Jul, the U.S. does not look like:
> three divisions minus X percent.

It looks like:
- some battalions still excellent;
- several merely serviceable;
- several gutted in leaders/specialists;
- a few effectively composite formations;
- artillery/engineer/signals systems patched with personnel transfers;
- fresh U1/U2 troops carrying disproportionate load;
- visible clusters of severe exhaustion / psychiatric casualties in the worst-hit units.

That is the correct organizational output of the casualty model.

# 15. Accounting rule

Going forward, every U.S. personnel-loss checkpoint should carry four columns:

1. **J-DIRECT** — killed / no credible RTD due Japanese action;
2. **CURRENT UNFIT** — temporary current manpower unavailable;
3. **FORMATION BREAKAGE** — F0/F1/F2/F3 battalion-equivalent distribution;
4. **LEADER/SPECIALIST/NP OVERLAY** — qualitative or bounded concentration.

Do not try to collapse all four into one “effective casualty” scalar.
