# 1. What TF58 can still do

## Fleet defense / protect Saipan
**GREEN**

TF58 can still:
- maintain fleet CAP;
- run western search;
- protect damaged/replenishment routes;
- coordinate with Lee;
- defeat a careless Japanese return attempt if given warning and concentration time.

This is its first priority.

## One concentrated offensive carrier effort
**GREEN-AMBER**

The 25-Jun working replay itself demonstrates that the surviving system can still generate:
- one large anti-surface wave;
- a second smaller coherent wave;
while sustaining CAP/search/recovery.

Therefore:
> **one serious offensive task at a time remains credible.**

By early/mid July, replenishment and new/reassigned decks improve this relative to 25 Jun.

## Routine island CAS / repeated suppression
**AMBER**

Possible, but expensive in opportunity cost.

Use:
- CVE;
- Aslito land-based air;
- NGFS;
- shore artillery

for routine Tinian/Saipan support.

Fast carriers should provide:
- selected deep suppression;
- fighter sweeps;
- reconnaissance;
- airfield neutralization;
not continuous tactical CAS by default.

## Deep western pursuit of the Japanese mobile fleet
**AMBER-RED**

Not impossible.

But:
- Japanese fleet remains alive;
- damaged U.S. carriers/escorts create route taxes;
- Saipan/Tinian/Guam amphibious shipping still needs protection;
- fast-carrier concentration margin is reduced.

Therefore do not center:
> TF58 races far west merely to seek a decisive battle.

Release only with:
- high-quality fleet contact;
- strong expected destruction payoff;
- acceptable Saipan/amphibious-cover residual.

## Cover one major amphibious operation
**GREEN-AMBER**

Yes.

TF58 can provide the fleet-cover/deep-suppression layer for:
- Tinian,
or
- Guam,

provided CVE / land air / old BB carry a substantial share of close support.

## Cover two near-simultaneous major amphibious operations
**RED**

This is the principal strategic loss.

TF58 no longer has a comfortable margin to:
- cover Tinian;
- cover Guam;
- suppress both island air systems;
- search west for the First Mobile Fleet;
- protect replenishment/damaged carriers;
- retain a strong offensive reserve

all at once.

# 2. Post-Saipan replacement/regeneration

Do not freeze TF58 at its 25-Jun damage state.

U.S. advantages:
- large replacement-aircraft pipeline;
- replacement pilots/crews;
- cross-deck ability;
- strong forward replenishment;
- repair ships / rear yards;
- new/reassigned carriers arriving into the theater.

Historical calibration:
Franklin reported for TG58 duty in mid-June and was conducting Guam/Rota strikes by 7 Jul.

Branch implication:
- new deck capacity enters the system;
- repaired light-deck casualties can return;
- air groups can be redistributed.

But:
- sunk hulls remain sunk;
- long-repair fleet carriers do not return on a few-day clock;
- deck crews / air-group cohesion / experienced leaders are not replaced instantaneously;
- screen/DD shortages and damaged-ship escort taxes remain.

Thus regeneration first restores:
**mission availability**
before it restores:
**historical surplus margin.**

# 4. Tinian / Guam consequence

TF58 damage does not force a complete operational halt.

It forces prioritization.

Tinian is attractive because:
- Saipan shore artillery can support;
- Aslito increasingly carries local aviation;
- CVEs can carry much tactical air;
- fast carriers can remain in fleet-cover / deep-suppression posture;
- Tinian seizure removes a major Japanese local ISR/air node.

Guam requires:
- larger oceanic transport cover;
- more carrier search/CAP;
- simultaneous concern about Tinian;
- more exposure to a returning Japanese mobile fleet.

Therefore the damaged-but-working TF58 condition strengthens:
**Tinian-first** relative to Guam-first.

---

# 5. Correct characterization

Do not say:
> “TF58 is crippled and cannot operate.”

Do not say:
> “TF58 has regenerated to normal by early July.”

Use:
> **TF58 remains a first-class combat fleet, but has lost the operational surplus that historically allowed it to perform several major missions in parallel.**

Its post-Saipan strength is sufficient for:
- fleet defense;
- one major offensive axis;
- one amphibious-operation cover package;

but insufficient for:
- unrestricted deep pursuit;
- simultaneous Guam + Tinian support;
- heavy routine CAS plus full western fleet defense.

That is the strategic value of the Japanese carrier success:
**not destruction of U.S. naval superiority, but destruction of U.S. concurrency.**
