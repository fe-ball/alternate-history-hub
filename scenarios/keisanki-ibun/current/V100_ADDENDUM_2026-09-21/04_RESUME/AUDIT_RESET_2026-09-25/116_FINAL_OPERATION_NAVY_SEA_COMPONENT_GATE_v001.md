# 1. Historical guard

Historical Saipan final attack was not purely an Army rifle attack.

The final mass included:
- Army combat troops;
- naval personnel;
- support personnel;
- walking wounded;
- MG/mortar/tank elements.

Contemporary/official U.S. reporting also confirms that in the final days:
- Japanese aircraft from other Marianas/Caroline bases continued small raids;
- Tinian shore batteries still shelled Isely/Aslito;
- local barge movement continued into July.

Therefore a joint local Army/Navy terminal action is historically grounded.

The Branch difference is:
**more of the surviving Navy contribution remains organized by function instead of dissolving into a generic human wave.**

# 2. Navy personnel ashore — YES, but role-selective

Saipan begins with a major Navy personnel population:
- base/port;
- air;
- AA/coastal-defense;
- communications;
- maintenance;
- construction/support;
- boat/harbor;
- command/staff.

By the final-operation gate, classify surviving naval personnel by whether their original system still has value.

## N1 — still-operating combat-system personnel
Examples:
- coastal/AA gun crews;
- surviving fire-control/observer/signals teams;
- working water-air support;
- boat-control/repair crews.

Rule:
**do not strip them early for infantry.**
Their system effect is worth more than one more rifleman.

## N2 — system-orphaned but combat-useful personnel
Examples:
- gun crews whose guns are destroyed;
- air-ground personnel with no remaining local airframe/support system;
- base-defense personnel displaced from lost facilities;
- selected construction/maintenance men no longer supporting a live system.

Rule:
**available for organized naval ground packets.**

Prefer:
- MG/automatic-weapon sections;
- demolition parties;
- route/security;
- close-assault teams;
- ammunition carriers;
- infiltration support.

Do not turn all N2 into anonymous infantry.

## N3 — high-value future cadre
Examples:
- radio/radar/fire-control specialists;
- rare aviation technicians;
- trained boat crews;
- selected staff/technical officers.

Rule:
- retain while Saipan system needs them;
- evacuate when future value exceeds remaining local combat value.

**do not mix all available sailors into the first-wave main body.**

Composition:

  - artillery position;

## Packet N-B — coastal infiltration
Composition:

Mission:

## Packet N-C — rear/escape security
Composition:
- remaining naval guards / support personnel.

Mission:
- protect final boat routes;
- protect wounded/cadre evacuation;
- hold a coastal pickup point;
- then either evacuate or join residual defense.

This preserves the bidirectional logic.

# 7. Local craft / “sea” maneuver — bounded YES

This is the most important maritime distinction.

By early July U.S. sea control around Saipan is very strong.

Historical July 4–5:
- about 200 Japanese attempted to leave the northwest coast in barges;
- U.S. artillery broke up the formation.

This proves:
- powered craft still existed;
- late movement was physically attempted;
- mass exposed boat movement was extremely dangerous.

Branch:
- did not spend the historical 13-craft 17-Jun loss;
- has better night pilotage/route planning;
- keeps craft dispersed;
- preserves trained crews.

Therefore a **small coastal maneuver** can be part of the final operation.

# 8. What the boat maneuver should be

Do not attempt:
- 200–500-man mass opposed landing;
- daylight coastal movement;
- a second main attack by sea.

Center mission:
**small low-signature one-way/one-cycle insertion.**

Purpose:
- place a demolition/infiltration packet behind or beside the Tanapag handoff seam;
- cut the coastal road/rail traffic;
- attack signals/FDC;
- create false impression of a wider penetration;
- assist escape/extraction after mission only if physically possible.

Planning scale:
**tens, not hundreds, as center.**

A low-hundreds sensitivity is possible only if:
- exact craft wallet closes high;
- U.S. radar/armed-craft gap is identified;
- pickup/landing point remains physically usable.

Do not book it now.

# 9. Why this maritime packet matters disproportionately

A 40–80-man packet does not change total assault manpower much.

But if it appears:
- behind the U.S. handoff seam;
- near route control;
- near artillery/FDC;
then it can force:
- rear-security diversion;
- road closure;
- duplicate contact reports;
- friendly-fire restrictions;
- uncertainty about whether the Japanese main breakthrough is wider than it is.

Therefore:
**maritime value is positional, not numerical.**

This fits the Branch counterlanding doctrine better than a late mass barge charge.
