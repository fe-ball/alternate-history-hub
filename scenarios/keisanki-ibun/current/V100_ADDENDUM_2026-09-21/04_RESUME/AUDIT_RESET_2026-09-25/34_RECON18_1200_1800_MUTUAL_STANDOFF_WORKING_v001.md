## 0. Core result

At noon both sides possess useful carrier contact:
- Japan has TF58 at ~10:42;
- the U.S. has the Japanese second echelon at ~10:35–10:50.

This does **not** automatically create a reciprocal carrier battle.

The decisive new fact is that the second echelon knows/strongly suspects it has been reported because U.S. scouts entered its CAP and transmitted before shadow was broken.

Selected result:
- Japan stops closing before entering a comfortable U.S. strike regime;
- the U.S. cannot close enough, quickly enough, without abandoning Saipan-cover geometry;
- both sides refresh contact during the afternoon;
- neither releases a normal anti-carrier strike through 18:00.

The outcome is an information-driven standoff.

## 1. Japanese decision at 12:05–12:20

Noon range:
- second echelon to TF58: **455–490 nm**;
- refueled first-line nucleus: **575–620 nm**.

Second-echelon staff assumes its position is compromised.

Selected movement:
- terminate the 20–22 kt eastward close;
- turn to an oblique WSW/holding course at **14–17 kt**;
- retain carrier flight-operation freedom and CAP;
- keep the second echelon as the threat-side layer rather than immediately collapsing west;
- first-line refueled nucleus also stops its eastward approach and follows at about **8–12 kt WSW/reorganization speed**;
- fast vanguard remains roughly 50–90 nm threat-side/east of the second echelon and preserves a surface/night option only if the U.S. actually comes west.

This converts the leapfrog from "advance until attack" into "advance until detected, then hold the threat."

## 3. U.S. recontact of the second echelon

The U.S. 10:35–10:50 carrier contact is also too old.

Selected relief:
- 8–12 carrier scouts/relief aircraft launched or retasked around **12:20–12:40**;
- around **13:45–14:00**, one pair recontacts the three-carrier second echelon;
- course now broadly WSW/oblique, not continuing the earlier eastward close;
- estimated range around **465–505 nm**;
- report transmitted before CAP breaks shadow.

The U.S. therefore sees that the Japanese carrier group has stopped closing.

A deeper continuation/search behind this group does **not** find the first-line refueled nucleus in the center case before 18:00.

The U.S. can infer another support/carrier layer may exist farther west but does not have an attack-quality fix on it.

## 4. Strike-gate comparison at 14:00

Available second-echelon MR after morning search/CAP:

Reason:
the second echelon is the last relatively clean carrier-air reserve; a strike from 465–500 nm would spend it for a low expected chance of decisive carrier damage, while merely maintaining the carrier threat already taxes U.S. CAP/search/deck cycles.

### U.S.

Range: roughly **465–505 nm**, Japanese group now opening/holding rather than closing.

A normal coherent anti-carrier strike would still require:
- long outbound transit;

- return into late-day/dark conditions;
- no forward friendly recovery field.

Selected release: **HOLD**.

The U.S. has ample aircraft strength, but not a good return geometry.

## 5. U.S. bounded westward pressure, not full chase

The U.S. tests whether the Japanese group will continue closing.

Selected 14:10–16:30 movement:
- TF58 makes a bounded westward station shift of roughly **12–16 kt along the threat axis**;
- still remains inside the broader Saipan protection system;
- no full high-speed pursuit.

Because the Japanese second echelon is also moving WSW at 14–17 kt, relative closure is small.

Resulting second-echelon range:
- ~15:00: **465–510 nm**;
- ~16:30: **470–520 nm**;
- ~18:00: **485–535 nm**.

The U.S. cannot force the range below the normal strike gate without committing to a genuine westward chase.

## 6. Fast surface vanguard and night-surface desire

The fast vanguard remains threat-side.

During the U.S. bounded westward shift:
- Japanese surface staff again opens an AMBER night-intercept calculation;
- unlike 16 Jun, the U.S. is moving somewhat west, but not fast or far enough.

By ~16:30:
- air/scout reports show TF58 is not executing a full pursuit;
- the surface option remains contingency only;
- no eastward night-attack course is ordered.

Better ISR creates the desire and then constrains it.

## 7. Reverse air rotation — Marianas survivors begin moving west

The air-ground-separation system can also work in reverse.

After repeated runway repair and servicing:
- selected carrier-qualified survivors in the Marianas can self-ferry west once they are not needed for immediate local defense/ISR.

Selected 18-Jun afternoon ferry:
- **8–12 aircraft** physically depart in small elements around 15:30–16:30;
- mix biased toward D4Y/B6N and a small number of long-range fighter aircraft with navigation lead;
- destination split between second echelon and the first-line support/reorganization system;
- no combat stores / no simultaneous strike role;
- arrival late afternoon/evening;
- not counted as immediate MR until landing inspection, fuel and host integration.

This is not new production.
It is the return of previously carrier-origin or carrier-qualified flying assets from the eastern air node to the western mobile node.

Operational effect:
- modestly improves the 19-Jun mobile-air regeneration prospect;
- correspondingly reduces Marianas local attack depth.

## 8. First-line replenishment/recovery during the standoff

Because the second echelon carries the visible threat:
- the refueled first-line nucleus does not need to close east;
- remaining first-line carriers have more time to complete top-off/maintenance queues.

Selected by 18:00:
- **3–4 first-line carriers** are at F-AMBER-GREEN / strong AMBER mission state;
- the remaining carrier is at least F-AMBER unless interrupted by ASW/oiler events;
- first-line shipborne MR across all five hosts recovers to roughly **86–108** through short repair/recombination, not new production;
- fighter MR roughly **42–52**;
- attack/recon MR roughly **44–56**.

The group still does not regain its pre-16-Jun 340–375 MR air mass because most of that flying layer was expended or displaced ashore.

## 9. U.S. afternoon task cost

No carrier strike does not mean inactivity.

Through 18:00 the U.S. pays for:
- anti-recon CAP;
- 8–12 relief/deep scouts;
- western CAP concentration against the visible second echelon threat;
- Saipan support tasks;
- ASW/SAR/recovery;
- readiness for a possible Japanese strike.

The Japanese carrier threat therefore consumes U.S. deck/fuel margin without firing.

The U.S. does **not** launch another heavy Marianas suppression package in this center interval because:
- the 17-Jun suppression already damaged the forward recovery system;
- the newly located Japanese carrier group is a more immediate operational threat;
- deck capacity is needed for search/CAP and contingency strike preparation.

## 11. Decision at 18:00

Japan:
- no carrier strike;
- preserve second echelon as visible forward threat;
- complete first-line refueling/reintegration;
- continue sparse Marianas ISR;
- maintain surface option only if U.S. turns into a real westward pursuit.

U.S.:
- no ultra-long late strike;
- maintain Saipan cover;
- continue recontact/search at reduced-but-persistent intensity;
- avoid being pulled far enough west to expose the lodgment and fixed support system.

This is a genuine mutual standoff produced by contact and range management, not a failure to act.
