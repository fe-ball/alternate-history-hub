# 0. Core judgment

The U.S. does not choose between:
1. continue full offensive immediately;
2. withdraw and rest.

The likely choice is:

> **operational pause ashore + continuous offensive containment at sea and in the air.**

Saipan becomes:
- a fortress;
- an air base;
- a logistics node;
- a gun platform against Tinian;
- a tripwire against Japanese fleet return.

The U.S. rests formations while refusing to give Japan a clean restoration window.

---

# 1. Why a partial withdrawal is unacceptable

After the Branch campaign the U.S. has observed:

- repeated Japanese fast reinforcement;
- successful surface-created relief windows;
- surviving Japanese carrier hulls;
- heavy battleship intervention;
- local Tinian/Guam aviation persistence;
- Japanese willingness to spend forces on suicidal but operationally useful actions.

Therefore U.S. command cannot safely assume:
> “Saipan is secure enough; most cover can leave.”

The danger is not only a literal Japanese recapture.

It is:
- night infiltration;
- local counterlanding;
- reinforcement of Tinian;
- renewed air attacks;
- surface bombardment;
- fleet-covered transport;
- a temporary isolation of Saipan while U.S. forces are being reorganized.

# 2. Threat hierarchy

## J-R1 — Tinian/local harassment
**HIGH / expected**

Includes:
- recon;
- small air attack;
- shore fire;
- night craft;
- infiltration;
- rescue/evacuation;
- movement of specialists and small troop packets.

This is the default continuing threat.

## J-R2 — bounded local counterlanding
**MEDIUM / operationally credible**

Scale:
- several hundred to low-thousands class,
if:
  - Tinian/local craft survive;
  - Japanese fleet creates a short corridor;
  - U.S. local cover is thinned.

Purpose need not be island recapture.

Possible missions:
- seize/destroy a support zone;
- hit Aslito;
- cut a road;
- recover trapped personnel;
- create a temporary lodgment for follow-on transport.

The U.S. must defend against this seriously.

## J-R3 — fleet-covered brigade-scale re-entry
**LOW-MEDIUM but high consequence**

A several-thousand-man transport / counterlanding attempt is physically conceivable if Japan combines:
- surviving fleet;
- transport hulls;
- Tinian/Guam ISR;
- a favorable U.S. fleet split.

But after Saipan is U.S.-held this is much harder than reinforcement of a friendly garrison:
- opposed landing;
- U.S. shore artillery;
- radar;
- local air;
- established beach/security system;
- no friendly receiving network.

Therefore not center, but cannot be dismissed.

## J-R4 — “tens of thousands from Bonins by shuttle”
**VERY LOW as an executable surprise recapture plan**

The U.S. may imagine the possibility in catastrophic planning,
but should not center it as the most likely Japanese course.

Reasons:
- Bonin/Volcano garrisons are themselves strategically valuable and being reinforced;
- oceanic troop lift is the binding constraint;
- repeated convoy movement is observable;
- Saipan is now an opposed landing problem;
- each cycle requires escort, unloading and surviving return shipping;
- U.S. aircraft/submarines/search can attack the route;
- Japanese carrier/surface cover cannot be permanently maintained over the corridor.

A tens-of-thousands transfer would require:
**a campaign, not a raid.**

The U.S. plans against the precursor:
- convoy concentration;
- fleet movement;
- Bonin air buildup;
- transport assembly;
not against 30,000 men appearing suddenly.

# 3. Bonin/Volcano threat — what is actually dangerous

The most dangerous northern contribution is initially not mass infantry.

It is:
- air staging;
- reconnaissance;
- replacement/ferry aircraft;
- medium/small transport;
- convoy assembly;
- submarine/air-sea reporting;
- a protected stepping-stone from Japan toward the Marianas.

Historically U.S. carrier forces attacked Iwo/Chichi while Saipan fighting was still underway.

Branch implication:
**Bonin suppression remains a real TF58 mission even after carrier losses.**

But it competes with:
- western fleet search;
- Tinian suppression;
- damaged-carrier protection;
- next-operation cover.

This is exactly why TF58 cannot do everything simultaneously.

## Ground
- mop up organized remnants;
- do not strip Saipan of healthy infantry;
- use F0/F1 and U1/U2 for security;
- remove F2/F3 from contact;
- create strong anti-counterlanding coastal/security sectors;
- retain mobile reserve around Aslito / Tanapag / western roads.

## Base
Maximum priority:
- Aslito air operations;
- radar / fighter direction;
- AA;
- fuel/ordnance;
- engineering;
- Tanapag clearance;
- port/shore unloading;
- roads;
- medical evacuation.

Goal:
> make every additional 24 h of U.S. possession harder to reverse.

## Naval
Do not withdraw TF58 as one body.

Instead:
- damaged hulls / exhausted elements -> rear;
- coherent carrier cores -> fleet cover;
- Lee -> surface-stopper function;
- CVEs / old BB / cruisers -> local support;
- DD screen -> search/ASW/damaged-ship protection.

## Air
Continuous:
- Tinian suppression;
- Guam/Rota watch;
- western search;
- northern Bonin/Volcano reconnaissance / periodic strike;
- ASW;
- local CAP.

# 5. U.S. 12–18 Jul operational pause

This is a **fighting pause**.

While ground formations reconstitute:

### Tinian
Escalating suppression:
- airfields;
- artillery;
- observation;
- boat facilities;
- communications.

### Saipan
Base construction accelerates.

### Bonins
Intermittent carrier / long-range attacks as mission capacity permits.

### Fleet
Replenish and repair in rotation.
Do not create one theater-wide period in which all fast carriers are absent.

### Ground reinforcement
- 77th movement continues;
- 3d Marine Division remains a high-value fresh formation;
- reconstitute selected 2d/4th Marine battalions;
- 27th/U1/U2 absorb Saipan security and recovery burden as practical.

# 6. Next-objective choice

The working center remains:
**TINIAN FIRST**

not because Tinian is easy,
but because leaving it alive continuously taxes Saipan.

Taking Tinian:
- removes the nearest Japanese aviation/ISR/artillery node;
- eliminates the easiest local counterlanding source;
- reduces daily CAP/search burden;
- lets Saipan artillery support the assault;
- permits shore-to-shore logistics;
- allows TF58 to stay primarily in fleet-cover/deep-suppression roles.

Guam remains important,
but can wait for:
- 77th mass;
- repaired amphibious package;
- better TF58 margin.

# 8. What would cause the U.S. to wait longer?

Delay Tinian if any two or more occur:

- Japanese mobile fleet attack-quality contact near the theater;
- TF58 coherent carrier cores fall below the one-operation cover floor;
- LVT / boat MR remains poor;
- selected Marine battalions remain F2/F3;
- Tinian defenses remain materially stronger than expected;
- U.S. intelligence sees a major Japanese convoy concentration;
- Aslito base output is still too fragile.

In this case:
> do not retreat;
> hold Saipan under heavy cover and suppress Tinian until the asymmetry improves.

---

# 9. What Japan can exploit during the pause

The dangerous Japanese strategy is not necessarily:
> immediately retake Saipan with 30,000 men.

A more realistic sequence is:

1. keep Tinian functioning;
2. maintain ISR;
3. make the U.S. keep carriers/BB/DD forward;
4. regenerate the mobile fleet westward;
5. move selected reinforcements/cargo into Tinian/Guam;
6. threaten a limited counterlanding or bombardment;
7. force the U.S. to spend its repaired TF58 capacity defensively;
8. attack only when U.S. next-operation shipping concentrates.

This can delay FORAGER even without retaking Saipan.

---

# 10. Final judgment

The U.S. post-Saipan center is:

> **hold hard, build fast, suppress continuously, repair in rotation, then eliminate Tinian before attempting to restore the full historical FORAGER tempo.**

The U.S. does not need to believe a 20,000–30,000-man Bonin counterinvasion is likely.

It only needs to believe:
> Japan retains enough fleet, air and transport capacity that weakening Saipan prematurely creates an unacceptable chance of a smaller but strategically disruptive re-entry.

That belief is strongly justified by the Branch campaign itself.
