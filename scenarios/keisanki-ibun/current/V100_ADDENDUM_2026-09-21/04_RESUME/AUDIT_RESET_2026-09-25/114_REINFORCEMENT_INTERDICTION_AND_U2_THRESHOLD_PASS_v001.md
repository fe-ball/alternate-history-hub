# 0. Core finding

A U.S. reinforcement battalion does not create useful U-R merely by reaching Saipan.

The actual conversion chain is:

> **release -> approach -> unload -> shore assembly -> road movement -> sector handoff -> relieved-unit disengagement -> reconstitution**

Japanese action can attack several links.

Therefore:
- U1/U2/U3 are not additive instant manpower;
- each reinforcement creates a new Japanese interdiction opportunity;
- interdiction delays the U.S. recovery curve;
- that delay can itself trigger the next reinforcement threshold.

But interdiction is not free:
- artillery ammunition;
- OP exposure;
- aviation sorties;
- night infantry;
- communications;
- infiltration forces
are consumed.

Repeated reinforcement therefore creates a **race of conversion vs attrition**, not a one-sided U.S. reinforcement ladder.

# 1. Historical geometry guard

Historical central Saipan / Tapotchau fighting remained a major battle through roughly 21–30 Jun.

Historical Aslito:
- physical possession came earlier;
- runway repair began immediately;
- limited aviation functions returned before the field became a mature rear-area base.

Branch:
- central high-ground / reverse-slope system is stronger;
- Aslito observed-fire denial lasts longer;
- Japanese local aviation outside Saipan survives qualitatively longer.

Therefore a late-June U1 reinforcement arrives during an overlap:

> **U.S. owns a southern logistics/base zone, but Japanese central observation/fire and night mobility still reach the conversion routes.**

This is the key reinforcement-interdiction geometry.

# 2. Reinforcement conversion stages

Track every U.S. reinforcement through six stages.

## R0 — release / approach
Force is still afloat.

Japanese possible actions:
- maritime ISR;
- D4Y/B6N confirmation/attack if available;
- night harassment;
- surface/submarine threat only if actually present.

Expected center:
- harassment / delay possible;
- sinking the whole reinforcement is not centered.

## R1 — unload / beach or harbor transfer
Force physically comes ashore.

Japanese possible actions:
- small air attack;
- long/medium artillery if a known handling area is still in reach;
- observation of traffic concentration;
- night shelling / nuisance attack.

Expected:
- direct destruction bounded;
- congestion, dispersion and unload interruption matter more.

## R2 — shore assembly
Weapons, radios, medical, ammunition, transport are sorted.

This is a high-value soft-system target.

Japanese action:
- registered artillery/mortar boxes;
- D4Y/water-air confirmation;
- night infiltration;
- road/fire-control harassment.

## R3 — road movement
Battalion moves from rear/landing area to relief sector.

Japanese target:
- road junctions;
- vehicle columns;
- bridge/defile;
- assembly points;
- route-control personnel.

This is one of the strongest Branch interdiction opportunities because file 39 already permits precomputed/renewed road and assembly target boxes.

## R4 — sector handoff
Fresh battalion takes over a line or holding sector.

Japanese action:
- fire during relief;
- local probes;
- infiltration;
- force both old and new battalions to remain simultaneously committed.

This is tactically more valuable than merely causing casualties:
> it can prevent the old battalion from disengaging.

## R5 — relieved unit reconstitution
Only now does U1 produce its full nonlinear benefit.

The value is:
- fresh battalion on line;
- old battalion removed;
- old battalion begins 36–72 h class first reconstitution.

If R4 is delayed, the entire U.S. recovery curve shifts right.

# 3. Japanese interdiction stack

Use four layers.

### JF-G — ground indirect fire
Best targets:
- roads;
- artillery/FDC;
- staging;
- handoff sectors;
- Aslito/service zones.

Requires:
- surviving gun/mortar;
- ammunition;
- current enough target box;
- OP/report path.

### JA — local aviation
Qualitative late capability only:
- D4Y/B6N small attack/recon;
- water-air observation;
- night harassment;
- fighter/recon denial.

Primary value:
- update target boxes;
- force dispersion;
- occasionally interrupt unloading/road movement.

Do not assign free large strike packages.

### JI — infiltration / local ground attack
Best use:
- road security;
- handoff seam;
- artillery/rear security;
- not a daylight assault on the landing beach.

### JM — coastal / boat mobility
Use:
- shift small Japanese packets around blocked roads;
- move observers/signals/ammunition;
- place infiltration groups where land approach is exposed.

Do not treat this as free opposed counterlanding.

# 4. Reinforcement delay bands

Old U.S. working clocks:
- first meaningful battalion effect: **12–24 h after safe landing**;
- relieved battalion first useful reconstitution: **36–72 h**.

Branch interdiction modifies the center.

## U1 first meaningful effect
If Japanese high-ground observation/fire remains coherent:

**~18–36 h class after physical landing**

not because the battalion is unable to fight,
but because:
- unload/assembly is dispersed;
- movement pauses;
- handoff is contested;
- the relieved battalion may not immediately disengage.

Stress-high:
**24–48 h class**
only if:
- J-F observation current;
- local aviation confirms routes;
- a meaningful night ground probe hits the handoff.

Do not use this as an automatic delay every time.

## Full rotation benefit
Starts only after:
- U1 has taken the sector;
- old unit is actually off line.

Thus the old unit's 36–72 h reconstitution clock starts **later than physical U1 landing**.

This is the key nonlinear effect.

# 5. Repeated reinforcement does not face identical resistance

Japanese ability to interdict each successive U.S. reinforcement decays.

### U1
Japanese system:
- strongest late-June remaining high-ground observation;
- more artillery/mortar coherence;
- more route knowledge.

Interdiction:
**highest marginal value.**

### U2
By the time U2 is sent:
- some OPs/guns are lost;
- U.S. routes are hardened/dispersed;
- U.S. learns from U1;
- but Japanese target knowledge of reinforcement procedures improves.

Interdiction:
**still meaningful, somewhat lower physical destruction, still useful for delay.**

### U3 / 77th
Later:
- Japanese coherent fire system is much weaker;
- U.S. landing/base system larger and better defended;
- 77th has more support mass.

Interdiction:
**mainly harassment / delay, not likely to prevent conversion unless an earlier Japanese threshold changed the campaign.**

Therefore do not impose a constant “+12 h Japanese tax” on every arrival.

# 8. Interdiction should target conversion, not troopship destruction

Center Japanese objective:

Not:
> sink William P. Biddle at all costs.

Instead:
> make the battalion take longer to become a reserve and prevent the battalion it replaces from leaving the line.

Priority:
1. observe unload/road movement;
2. hit staging/route junctions;
3. hit the handoff sector;
4. force old and new units to overlap;
5. hit artillery/FDC/vehicles supporting the relief;
6. use night infiltration to delay route security.

This is much more compatible with surviving Japanese capabilities.

A rare transport hit remains a branch event, not the center.

Aslito should not be binary.

- emergency recovery / patrol can exist;
- limited fighter/tactical cycles may exist;
while:
- support parks;
- roads;
- fuel/ammo handling;
- control;
- dispersal
remain under intermittent Japanese observation/attack.

Therefore Aslito helps U1:
- local air cover;
- liaison;
- casualty/recon;
- some support.

But it does not erase reinforcement friction.

Branch Japanese fire/air effort should increasingly target:
- the **base-support system**
rather than runway pavement itself.

# 10. Japanese cost of blocking reinforcement

This is mandatory.

Every U1/U2 interdiction effort debits:
- artillery/mortar ammunition;
- observer exposure;
- wire/radio maintenance;
- aviation fuel/airframes/crews;
- infiltration troops;
- night movement capacity.

Thus successful delay can accelerate J decline.

This produces a second feedback:

> Japan can delay U.S. reconstitution, but by doing so may bring its own final-operation gate closer.

Therefore repeated interdiction is not a free way to stretch the campaign indefinitely.

# 11. Final-operation timing feedback

The sequence can become:

1. U1 is released.
2. Japan expends fire/air/night assets to delay U1 conversion.
3. U1 still eventually takes over a sector.
4. U2 is considered/released because rotation is still poor.
5. Japanese command observes that a second fresh reserve is entering.
6. Remaining Japanese ammunition/C2 has already been spent on U1 interdiction.
7. Waiting further now gives:
   - stronger U.S. U-R;
   - weaker Japanese J-F/J-C.

This creates a natural T-FA trigger.

### Direction
A stronger Japanese defense can therefore generate:
- **more U.S. reinforcements**
and simultaneously
- **an early-July final attack that is not much later than history**.

The two are causally connected.

# 12. Expected final-operation improvement after reinforcement interdiction

Japanese attacks before U2 fully converts can find:
- fresh unit moving/just deployed;
- relieved unit not yet reorganized;
- support routes congested;
- reserve command structure in transition.

This is a better target than attacking a fully stabilized U.S. front.

Therefore final attack quality improves through **timing against a rotation seam**, not merely greater Japanese manpower.

Preferred axis selection should use:
- identified handoff seam;
- route/FDC node;
- reserve assembly;
- Aslito/support junction.

This is a stronger mechanism than generic “more banzai troops.”

# 13. What this changes about casualties

U.S. incremental loss increases not only because more Japanese troops attack.

The additional mechanism is:
- fresh reinforcements enter observed routes;
- old units remain exposed longer;
- both old and new units can be in the same sector during contested handoff;
- rear/support personnel are pulled into security/close combat.

Thus U.S. casualty increment should include a higher share of:
- route/security;
- signals/FDC;
- junior leaders;
- transport/vehicle personnel
than a simple frontline attrition model.

Japan pays:
- observer/artillery specialists;
- infiltration infantry;
- aviation crews/aircraft;
- mortar/artillery ammunition
before the final operation.
