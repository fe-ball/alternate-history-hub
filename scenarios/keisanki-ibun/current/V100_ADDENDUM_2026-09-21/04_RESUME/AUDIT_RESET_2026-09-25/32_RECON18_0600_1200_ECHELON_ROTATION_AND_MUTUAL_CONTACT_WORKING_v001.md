## 0. Core question

After partial refueling and role rotation, which Japanese carrier layer should face east on 18-Jun morning, and who reacquires whom first?

Selected answer:
- the **second carrier echelon** becomes the threat-side carrier layer at dawn because it has the cleaner fuel/air-group state;
- a **2–3-carrier first-line refueled nucleus** follows farther west;
- Marianas reconnaissance again reacquires TF58 first;
- U.S. deep search subsequently finds the fast surface vanguard and then the second carrier echelon, but not the first-line refueled nucleus by noon.

Thus fuel rotation recovers operational margin but transfers exposure from the depleted first line to the fresher second echelon.

## 7. The fuel-rotation trade

This is the cost of the leapfrog.

Fuel benefit:
- second echelon can stay forward without consuming the partially replenished first-line nucleus;
- first-line carriers continue completing fuel/maintenance while preserving an operational nucleus.

Information/exposure cost:
- U.S. scouts encounter the second echelon first and can identify it as a carrier formation;
- the previously "clean reserve" is no longer hidden.

Thus role rotation does not create free endurance; it transfers both duty and detection risk.

## 10. Interpretation

The 18-Jun morning is no longer a simple repetition of 17 Jun.

Fuel rotation changes the observable order:
- on 17 Jun the U.S. found the fast vanguard and struggled to find the first-line carrier core;
- on 18 Jun the forward role is held by the fresher second echelon, so the U.S. eventually finds **that** carrier group first.

Japan still wins the initial TF58 recontact race because Marianas is close to the U.S. operating region.

But by noon both sides possess a useful carrier contact:
- Japan sees TF58;
- U.S. sees the Japanese second echelon.

Neither yet has a comfortable normal strike solution.

The next gate is therefore a genuine mutual approach/hold decision, not a predetermined Japanese first strike.
