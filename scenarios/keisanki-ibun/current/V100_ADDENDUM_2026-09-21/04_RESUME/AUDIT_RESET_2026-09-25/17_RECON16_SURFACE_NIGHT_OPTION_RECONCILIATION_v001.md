# 16 June surface-night option reconciliation — repeated contact creates desire, geometry kills execution

## 0. Question

After the revised reconnaissance chain (15:28, 16:15, 18:08 U.S.-carrier-region observations), should the longer contact history retroactively create a Japanese desire for a night surface attack?

Answer:
- **YES at the level of planning, posture and option preservation.**
- **NO at the level of an actual eastward night-attack order in the selected geometry.**

The missing item was not a surface battle. It was the command process that tests one.

## 1. Existing structural reason the option must be considered

The current working command framework already separates:
- First Fleet / night-surface system;
- Second Fleet / heavy fast-surface strike;
- fast surface/recon vanguard;
- heavy surface support.

The current working sea-geometry hypothesis places the fast surface vanguard about 30–60 nm threat-side/east of the first-line carriers before the afternoon withdrawal. Modern surface-strike DD fits preserve Type 93 night/cruiser-DD attack value and, where fitted/trained, second effective salvos.

Therefore repeated usable U.S.-carrier observations should trigger a night-intercept solution. Ignoring that possibility would underuse an existing Branch capability.

## 2. What is inserted retroactively

### 15:38–15:45 after the first afternoon carrier report
The shore original and Oyodo amplification make it reasonable for the mobile-force staff to open a NIGHT-SURFACE OPTION.

Order state:
- do **not** reverse east immediately;
- fast vanguard changes from simple maximum WSW retirement to **forward hold / elastic withdrawal**;
- maintain a threat-side separation and preserve fuel for a possible night dash;
- heavy support remains behind/adjacent rather than committing all heavy units;
- prepare projected intercept solutions for several U.S. courses;
- require another contact or evidence of U.S. westward movement before attack release.

### 16:24–16:30 after the second afternoon report
The option remains open, but the report does not show U.S. westward pursuit.

The U.S. is still operating in the Marianas/Saipan protection region. The fast vanguard therefore preserves the night option rather than charging east.

### 18:08 recontact
The new report is tactically valuable, but it makes the intercept geometry worse rather than better:
- the U.S. carrier group remains in/near the Marianas support region;
- no meaningful westward chase is observed;
- Japanese carriers and surface layers have been opening the sea gap for hours.

This is the point where a surface commander has a **strong desire to exploit**, but the actual intercept solution remains unattractive.

## 3. Geometry sensitivity using the selected local-W case

Document 11 selected at 14:15:
- representative U.S. group approximately 346 nm from Box 1;
- fast vanguard working position 30–60 nm east of Box 1.

That implies a rough vanguard-to-U.S. range of about **287–317 nm at 14:15** if the separation lies largely along the threat axis.

To preserve the option, use a forward-hold W sensitivity:
- vanguard WSW speed 18–22 kt rather than simply matching the carrier 26–28 kt withdrawal;
- U.S. map displacement follows the selected local-W case;
- exact named-hull speed/formation remains open.

Resulting vanguard-to-U.S. ranges:
- about **329–364 nm at 15:44**;
- about **351–389 nm at 16:30**;
- about **397–442 nm at 18:08**;
- about **436–486 nm at 19:30**.

These are not historical surveyed positions. They show the direction of the geometry.

At 15:44, even a 30-kt eastward dash requires about 11–12 h to a stationary 330–360 nm target. If the U.S. group itself continues eastward by roughly 10 kt, practical relative closure is much worse. A same-night intercept therefore already depends on the U.S. holding position or coming west.

By 18:08 the night attack is decisively unattractive unless the U.S. reverses west.

## 4. Why repeated contact does not imply attack

Longer contact improves confidence in:
- U.S. operational presence;
- rough region;
- the fact that carrier aviation remains active.

But it also improves confidence in a negative fact:
- **the U.S. is not charging west into the Japanese night-surface system.**

Thus better ISR creates two competing effects:
1. desire/opportunity recognition rises;
2. bad geometry is identified earlier and with greater confidence.

In this case effect 2 wins.

This is exactly the Branch logic desired: better information does not automatically make commanders more aggressive; it lets them distinguish an attractive night intercept from a costly blind thrust.

## 5. Fuel and formation consequence

The retroactive insertion does have a small physical consequence.

Because the fast vanguard does not simply mirror the carrier body's maximum WSW retirement after ~15:40:
- vanguard-carrier separation grows;
- at 18–19h it can plausibly be roughly 50–100 nm threat-side in the selected sensitivity instead of staying fixed at 30–60 nm;
- the vanguard spends somewhat more high-speed/option-preservation fuel than a pure retirement track;
- it remains more exposed than if immediately absorbed into carrier screens.

Do not double-count this as a free future eastward starting position. The fuel and separation must be carried into the night/oiler ledger.

## 6. Decision at 19:30

Latest actual U.S.-carrier observation: 18:08.

Selected surface decision:
- **NO eastward night-attack course.**
- Keep the fast vanguard organizationally intact and threat-side for several more hours while watching for a U.S. westward move or a submarine/air report that changes the geometry.
- If no such report appears, close the immediate intercept option and continue elastic WSW withdrawal/rejoin toward the broader fleet support geometry.
- Do not absorb the vanguard instantly into carrier screens; its night-battle role remains available for the next opportunity.

This reconciles the revised reconnaissance with the older working logic that the night-surface option exists but closes when the U.S. does not move west.
