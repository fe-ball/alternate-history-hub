# 5. Geometric character of the fight

The U.S. retains:
- better group-level coordination;
- F6F dive/energy performance;
- experienced carrier fighter-direction doctrine;
- numerical initiative;
- ability to refuse low-value turning fights.

Japan has:
- warning;
- local altitude opportunity for part of CAP;
- multiple fighter types;
- shorter recovery distance;
- AA sanctuary effect near the fields;
- improved track/vector discipline.

Therefore center geometry is:

1. F-A makes first intercept / classification;
2. U.S. forward/high elements engage;
3. F-B enters during first large merge;
4. U.S. attempts to retain some fighters above the main melee;
5. F-C enters lower/later as the fight approaches island airspace;
6. surviving U.S. fighters must decide:
   - continue fighter hunt;
   - strafe;
   - attack airfield/AA opportunities;
   - disengage before fuel/formation state degrades.

No instant one-pass annihilation is selected.

# 6. Island-defense coupling

The critical new rule:

> **Japanese fighters are not fought over neutral empty sea.**

As the engagement approaches Tinian:
- U.S. low/medium-altitude pursuit enters heavy/medium AA opportunity;
- damaged Japanese fighters can dive toward defended recovery zones;
- U.S. pilots must decide whether to chase through AA;
- low-level strafing after the fighter fight exposes aircraft to 25-mm / light AA.

Therefore air-to-air and ground-fire loss channels must be resolved sequentially, not with one combined kill rate.
