## 1. No numbers until "36 Daihatsu" can truly reach the assault anchorage

### 1.1 Class physics

Historical standard **14-m Daihatsu**: around **14.3 m long**, load draft around **0.75–0.8 m**, **7.5–8 knots**, typical practical nominal **70 men OR 10 t cargo OR one 7.4/7.5 t Type 95 tank** (different variants exist). This is **exclusive maximum load alternatives**, not 70 soldiers **and** 10 t supplies. Deck space, gun wheel load, tide, soil and crew/sea-state reduce actual unloading. A heavy Type 97 medium tank is **NOT** one-boat payload for ordinary 14m Daihatsu. References: https://www.navypedia.org/ships/japan/jap_aux_lc.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daihatsu-class_landing_craft ; U.S. Navy USNI contemporary observation, Nov 1942 https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1942/november/boats-japanese-landing-force (wartime capacity estimates sometimes overestimate; use 70 people rather than article's 120).

For **36 boats actually launched and usable**:
- all-troop mathematical one-wave maximum: **36 × 70 = 2,520 men, zero heavy cargo**;
- all-cargo mathematical one-wave maximum: **36 × 10 = 360 t, zero troops**;
- **NOT 2,520 men plus 360 t**;
- each large gun/vehicle boat consumes one troop/cargo boat's simultaneous capacity, and gun crew/ammunition/traction must be landed as well.

## 2. Beach physics: Sandwip Channel is not a fixed 1943 sandy beach

Modern International Hydrographic Review Bangladesh survey report (2016) identifies **predominantly semidiurnal tides**, and states tidal range can **exceed 7 metres near the Sandwip channel**, with gently sloping coastline, sediment transport and site-specific variations. **This is a modern regional warning, NOT a 1943-12 exact Sitakunda tidal prediction or 7 m every day**. Sources https://ihr.iho.int/articles/bathymetric-survey-in-the-coastal-area-of-bangladesh-a-challenging-experience-for-the-surveyor/ and https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/ihr/article/download/25817/29965

Because large troop/transports and Shinshu/Akitsu have **deep ship drafts**, they must anchor outside the nearshore mud flats at a **surveyed safe depth**. Shallower ~0.8m Daihatsu still can ground far from dry ground at ebb and trap vehicle wheels in soft sediment. First-wave movement from wet intertidal flats to all-weather inland route requires engineered track/corduroy/mats, bridging, tow teams, and a safe exit above high-water line.

- **Banshbaria–Sitakunda northern candidate:** objectives west→east exit to railway and north trunk road; potentially cuts Chittagong–Feni main line, but strong ebb mudflats/7m-class regional tidal environment and British RAF close fields; no confirmed 1943 assault beach survey.
- **Kumira–Sonaichhari intermediate northern alternative:** closer to Chittagong city and port but also closer to city defense and airfields. Railway threat remains, mobility/boat unloading not yet verified. NO automatic better landing ground.
- **Anwara/Karnaphuli south-bank option:** threatens port-mouth and southern front communications, not direct northern railway severance. Far closer to British port and observation/air, requires river-current/crossing and is a different operation, not a free follow-on.

At this stage **do not close any site**. The map geometry establishes goal, not accessible WWII wet-sand bearing strength, inland route/bridge status, hydrographic soundings or British coastal watching. Modern visitor beach pictures/2017 piers are not evidence that the same man-made installations existed in 1943.

**Go/No-go before beach naming:** obtain period Admiralty/British India coastal hydrographic chart or plausible contemporary survey and at least 2–3 viable high tide approaches, cross-shore bearing test, measured unloading time, 1943 road/rail junction exits and local garrison/RAF alert, plus fallback beach if ebb strands craft. A higher sea-level range can **make the first tide a one-wave tactical window**, not automatically allow two night waves.

### 3.1 Turnaround and worst-case bottleneck

**Pure craft leg example ONLY:** 3–6 nautical miles from anchored mother hulls to shore **at ~8 kt** implies **~23–45 minutes one-way**, before forming waves, grounding, hand unloading, relaunching, and hoisting/loading guns from ships. Two transits already consume ~45–90 minutes sailing time **per round trip**; crew, surf, tide and crane/boat approach can add multiple hours. This is not a calculated local 1943 anchorage distance — **anchorage must be chart-qualified**.

**Operational delivery envelopes, not measured battle outcomes**:
- **Short one-tide window:** ~**1,100–1,550 troops; ~60–100t ordinary stores; ~2–4 gun packages** *if most of first wave manages to land*, no repeat round assumed.
- **Working first 12–24h:** ~**1,800–2,300 troops; ~130–200t stores; ~4–6 gun packages**, requiring first and part of second wave plus some follow-up unloading in a weather and RAF-permitted window.
- **Ceiling for two clean complete waves:** **2,800 troops, 240t stores, 8 gun/vehicle packages**, *not guaranteed merely by H+24h*.
- **>3,500–5,000 troops by H+24h** requires roughly a third or more mixed wave, extra independent craft or a captured serviceable berth; **NOT the central estimate** in a watched tidal coast under nearby RAF.
- **H+48/72h to build 4,000–5,000 lodged troops** is conditional on keeping ships/boats alive, sufficient night/daylight transfer windows, established AA/fighter cover and no British reserves closing the beach exits. If daytime seaborne access fails, the first night's force may become a stranded raid.

### 3.2 Smaller alternative if mother-ship commitment fails

If **only Akitsu's 20 proven barges** (and no separately certified additional 16) can be concentrated: use e.g. **12 personnel×70 = 840**, **4 cargo×10 = 40t**, **2 gun packages**, **2 engineer cargo×10 = 20t** **per ideal first wave**. This can *raid* and disrupt a rail segment, but **cannot automatically fracture the complete British southern line or force surrender of Chittagong**. A second full wave gives 1,680 troops + 120t + 4 gun packages in favorable repeated operations, still hard to sustain against British 26th Brigade reserve.

### 3.3 Ship cargo and H+72 support

- Four or five nominal transports can carry considerably more than the first tide can unload. **"Cargo embarked" is never "cargo ashore".**
- A landing force of roughly 2,000 men needs at least **~8 tonnes/day of 4L/man water** before cooking/washing; rations ~2–4 tonnes/day are an illustrative mass budget, plus shells, mortars, small-arms ammunition, fuel, radio batteries, medical care, AA and repairs. **Intense combat may consume tens of additional tonnes/day**. This is a model baseline, not an audited Japanese staff consumption table.
- Initial 130–200t may include construction and heavy kit, not all consumables. The H+72 obligation is a **replenishment path and evacuation capability**; total cargo alone cannot prove sustainable bridgehead or supply rail/interior fights.
- Boats surf-stranded/damaged/being repaired consume the count and reduce next day's deliverable people or tons; under RAF strikes the actual operational number may fall **below the 36 nominal**. A second landing on a different shore cannot reuse those same boats at the same hour.

**No automatic result:** Under defensive British D2+D3 center, a one-night J0 raid may interfere with rails but **cannot realistically guarantee a lasting breach**. J1 **can create serious shock** if tide, unloading and RAF suppression align, but it is far from certain and may suffer high shipping/infantry losses when the British 26th/RAF reacts. J2 massive cover may make Britain call for greater RN and US responses but is an expensive separate commitment.

**Intelligence model:** Both sides remember 1942; Britain watches north coast but cannot watch every mud flat every tide. Japan can deceive scout reports but cannot assume all RAF fighter response remains asleep. British HQ can misjudge the main Japanese landing objective, Japan can misjudge how quickly 26th moves. Outcome follows logistics/tactical facts, not commander stupidity.
