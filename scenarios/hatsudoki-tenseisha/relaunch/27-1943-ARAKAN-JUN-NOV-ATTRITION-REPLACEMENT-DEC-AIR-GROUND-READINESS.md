## 2. Disease is a snapshot, not a killed-in-action total

- Official British Army medical history reports devastating malaria in 1943 Burma; a 1944 US Army analysis of 1943 IV Corps hospital-admission charts estimates peak summer **~120 per 1,000 strength temporarily noneffective** if each admission removes three weeks of duty. XV Corps's early experience was described as similar. This is a planning/calibration indicator, **not a recorded R3 XV Corps point estimate**. Sources:
  - https://www.generalstaff.org/WW2/Hist_UK/ArmyMedicalServicesCampVol5.pdf, Malaria chapter/graph 69.
  - https://www.generalstaff.org/BBOW/LOV-DUP/S7_Health_31-OCT-1944.pdf.
- Conditional R3 British active-front illness snapshot during peak monsoon: **8–15% unfit**, and by Dec **3–7%** among personnel forward exposed to jungle disease. These are **R3 model parameters**; local medical discipline, mepacrine, rotation and shipping hospitals control the rate.
- Japanese jungle/exposed troops might face **10–18% peak and 5–10% Dec** temporarily unfit in this model, but no local validated R3 disease returns exist. The suggested Japanese disadvantage reflects constrained medical/forward coastal support, **not a proved 1943 unit rate**.
- **Example only:** For **25,000 British exposed** field/corps-support personnel, Dec 3–7% = **750–1,750** temporarily unfit; for **17,000 Japanese exposed**, Dec 5–10% = **850–1,700**. These are **illustrations based on arbitrary exposure denominators** and are NOT additional division casualties. If known exposed headcounts differ, replace the examples.
- Medical sick temporarily off duty on Dec 10 and a wounded casualty still hospitalized can overlap; when calculating the true daily unavailable strength, apply illness percentage to the **otherwise medically available cohort**, not to people already listed missing or wounded.
