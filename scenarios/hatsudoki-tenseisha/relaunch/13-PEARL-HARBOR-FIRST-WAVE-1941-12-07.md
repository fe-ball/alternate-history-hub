## 1. Kido Butai aircraft state

Carrier count, deck/ hangar capacity, crew strength and broad strike organization remain near historical values. Owari does not create more carriers.

Main R2 differences:
- fighter arm is far stronger
- torpedo bomber is a somewhat better-developed late B5N, not a new generation
- dive bomber remains D3A-class
- fleet train / repair / replacement / search weaknesses are still broadly historical

### First-line carrier fighter — 昴二一零戦

Late-1941 Kido Butai standard:
- O4 / 昴二一 1600PS
- normal combat mass about 3.03t
- max about 615km/h center
- 5000m about 4:05–4:15
- armor behind pilot / head
- partial self-sealing / fuel-fire protection
- practical radio + DF
- **2×20mm wing + 2×13.2mm synchronized nose**
- 20mm roughly 80–100 rpg class
- 13.2mm roughly 200–250 rpg class
- 2×60kg standard fighter-bomber load
- centerline 300–330L-class drop tank
- 250kg center bomb is special/growth, not routine Pearl Harbor load

The aircraft retains the Zero family’s low-speed maneuverability, climb, acceleration, visibility and carrier handling while substantially improving speed, sustained firepower, radio coordination and survivability. It is not merely “a faster Zero.”

### B5N / D3A

- B5N late standard remains the torpedo/horizontal bomber; installation/reliability somewhat improved, not replaced by B6N
- D3A late standard remains the dive bomber; essentially 1941-class
- B6N and D4Y are not combat-deployed in the opening strike

## 2. First-wave organization

Use broadly historical first-wave size and target logic:
- about 183 aircraft
- torpedo / level-bomb / dive-bomb / fighter roles broadly unchanged
- stronger fighters do not replace the torpedoes or 800kg armor-piercing bombs needed against the battleship line

The battleship-line strike result is therefore expected to remain broadly close to history; the largest R2 difference is airfield suppression and air combat.

## 3. Airfield suppression

Key logic:
- historical 7.7mm strafing already caused severe ground-aircraft losses
- R2 13.2mm + larger 20mm ammunition reserve increases **same-day mission kills** of parked aircraft, vehicles and support equipment
- do not multiply total burned aircraft without limit; the main effect is that aircraft that might historically have been repairable within hours are more often disabled for the day

Working first-wave effect:
- Wheeler/Bellows immediate fighter recovery is materially worse
- Haleiwa remains an important hole because it was not initially suppressed in the same way
- same-day repairable/ready fighter pool after first wave is roughly **30–40% lower than a historical-style first-wave recovery pool**, without asserting that every damaged aircraft is destroyed

## 4. Air combat

Do **not** model the fight as “615km/h automatically wins.”

R2 Zero advantages are cumulative:
- much higher top speed
- strong power/weight and re-acceleration
- excellent low-speed turn
- strong climb/vertical options
- mature carrier handling
- improved radio coordination
- heavier 13.2mm + 20mm burst
- armor and partial fuel protection

US fighters retain real options:
- P-40 high-speed dive and ruggedness
- P-36 can be locally dangerous in low-speed maneuver
- surprise/high-energy first passes can still kill Japanese bombers or fighters
- Japanese low-altitude strafing still exposes aircraft to ground fire

Haleiwa-based fighters can still get airborne and obtain local successes. The R2 difference is that repeated re-engagement is much harder: the Zero can disengage, climb, re-accelerate and return, while radio reports can bring nearby fighter sections toward the contact.

**CLOSED central first-wave air-combat state:**
- US effective airborne fighter resistance during the wave: roughly **6–10 aircraft-class**
- US fighters destroyed/forced out of combat during the wave: roughly **3–5**
- Japanese aircraft shot down by US fighters during the first wave: roughly **1–3**, not zero
- by first-wave end, **no coherent organized US CAP** remains, though individual aircraft/pilots may still be airborne or recoverable

## 5. Japanese first-wave losses

Historical-style first-wave losses are reduced but not erased.

Working central:
- R2 Zero air-combat loss: 0–1
- additional Zero loss to ground fire/other causes: 0–1
- torpedo/dive-bomber losses from AA and low-level exposure remain real
- **total first-wave Japanese non-return: roughly 5–7 aircraft**

This is a bounded audit estimate, not an exact immutable count.
