## 3. Exhaust/engine technology diffusion: what exactly is meant

[11] 1941 R3 technology already includes **rearward low-backpressure exhaust**, tuned exits, **ejector exhaust + cooling-flow entrainment**, oil/cowling/prop integration and measurement. [18] has radio EMI grounding and 1943 maintenance methods. [20] projected **1942H2 昴二二 waterplane engine/prop integration** and Kyofu Kasei cooling/exhaust/QC propagation.

**Do NOT confuse three different outputs:**
1. **engine shaft power** (actual crankshaft PS, needs certified engine bench/supercharger figure),
2. **net propulsive exhaust thrust** (exhaust stacks and ejector, depends airspeed/geometry),
3. **cooling-drag reduction** (cowl airflow / CHT margin), and independently **engine TBO/corrosion**.

In particular **ejector exhaust nozzle does NOT make a Kinsei/Kasei engine's published 1,300PS become 1,500PS**; the third-party maker keeps engine identity. It may modestly improve whole-aircraft speed, climb cooling/ready rate only after iterative engine/airframe test. Historical A6M5 Model 52 used improved ejector stacks in **late 1943**, with cowl-flap and heat shield challenges; sources https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_A6M_Zero (Model52 notes and disputed early production chronology), https://airpages.ru/eng/jp/a6m5.shtml.

On floats, first-issue hotspots:
- hot exhaust near **water spray / wing and float attachment**, salt corrosion, maintenance cycles, gas flow strikes own fuselage/cowling, any leaking exhaust into intake/cockpit;
- propeller/spray clearance if high-power Subaru changes prop diameter;
- CHT high while taxiing on hot windless water, cooling under climb and out of water;
- exhaust heat protection for thin skin and fuel system, soot/blackout/engine visibility in catapult operations.

Thus **1942 R3 A0** may gain more from **reliable 1,500PS, lower cooling drag and radio** than from a fantastically large standalone ejector speed bonus. **A1 1943** can layer refined coolers and exhaust without counting that as a new engine family. For E13A/F1M/H8K and Kyofu, instrumentation and factory know-how spreads gradually through manufacturer instruction and squadron workshops; not every 1942 service airframe gets all modifications simultaneously.

## 4. Real operational value in the pinch — time-critical tasks, not permanent fighter equivalence

**Why Rufe works in R3 where its historical career was curtailed:**
- Midway/Port Moresby/Solomons/remote islands held/used for longer → Japan has more **coastal forward observation posts, boat bases and partially finished runways** to defend/cover, so afloat interceptors fill a persistent niche.
- Shifting **Chitose** (and possibly Chiyoda) to retained seaplane support vs historical carriers maintains qualified repair/depot/crews and ferry operations. Early local dispersal prevents a *single cratering* from erasing all Japanese airborne scouts or an emergency fighter scramble.
- **Radio/DF and warning relays** can ensure 2–5 aircraft launch before second raid instead of receiving an enemy strike with moored airplanes; [14] MO 4–5 + deck fighter 10–12 is precisely a combined-capability example.
- **Operational reserve:** preserve land-carrier Zero detachments for real fleet air superiority, while float fighters deal with unescorted scouts, slow bombers, vulnerable strike remnants, amphibious boats and low-altitude patrol. Escort a reconnaissance plane or protect a small forward repair/airfield-construction team.

**Hard no-go zones:**
- A floatplane moored at water's edge without dispersed fuel, local watch, tug, salvage and pilot/engine crew can be **destroyed in first wave**; it does not scramble just because it has floats.
- All fighters in R3 may be stronger, so **float Zero remains disadvantaged versus contemporary high-performance RAF Spitfires and US fast carrier fighters**, especially at high altitude, despite new engine. A six-plane base cannot provide 24-hour fleet-CAP or protect big transports from mass daylight strike.
- **Flying-boat rescue & ISR / emergency CAP are different functions**. A small Rufe section is often more valuable as an alert/retaliatory *margin* than continuous air superiority. Surface warship AA / ground radar / fighter direction remains required.
- On an amphibious night assault, the relevant float fighter **can defend rear moorings, relay/delay local daylight attacks**, but *not* lift from a moving Hyūga on a routine combat catapult; [22]/ [32] exclude unqualified shipboard A6M2-N/N1K1 routine CAT launch.

- R3 May MO: **6 physical at Rekata**, **4–5 launched/arrived** in [14] implies **67–83% committed in that response**. This ratio is a *scenario event*, not general constant serviceability; 5 physically ready need warning, surface conditions and ~time to take off.

If (illustration only) fighters have ~70–80% mission readiness, **~5–6 combat-available fighters** scattered across multiple bases; no one squadron 8 immediately over the landing or RAF field.
- R3 March 1943 [18]: **90–120** Japanese national A6M2-N/N1K aircraft *combat-capable pool*, **55–75 first-line** **CONDITIONAL PROVISIONAL**, not a known factory output and not a Bengal pool. It is also not an extra 90–120 on top of national serviceable fighter counts.

### 4.2 Counter-air doctrine in three windows

1. **Before a threat / camouflage:** surface patrol and local watchers, engines warmed, pre-flown water lanes, aircraft dispersed among coves, fuel/guns/radio batteries separate, prepared salvage.
2. **First strike after detection:** try to launch warning patrol or scatter; **expect partial airframe losses**, not guaranteed warning. Night/sea-state may preclude a sortie.
3. **Second strike / following dawn:** if surviving, **~2–5 float fighters** can close one gap, protect scout/repair/boat convoy, complicate second enemy attack until fleet/land Zero fighters join, and provide a continued warning network despite runway damage. Can delay enemy tempo **without** defeating mass incoming bombers or Spitfire wing.
